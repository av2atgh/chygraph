\chapter{Colouring}
\label{ch:colouring}
\index{graph colouring|textbf}

This chapter and the next complete Part~III's third family.
Chapter~\ref{ch:hittingset}
minimised a set subject to a covering constraint; Chapter~\ref{ch:cover}
tightened the constraint and found the algorithm that solves it. Colouring
keeps the hard constraint and drops the minimisation: there is nothing to
optimise, only a question of whether a configuration exists at all.

It is also where Chapter~\ref{ch:potts}'s claim is tested. Proper colouring is
the antiferromagnetic $q$-state Potts model at zero temperature --- this book's
own model at the opposite sign --- so if the machinery is what it has been
claimed to be, the colouring calculation should be a substitution and not a new
theory. It is.

The chapter runs in three steps. Section~\ref{sec:colouringdef} states the
problem, and states it twice, because a complex admits two constraints where an
edge admits one: every member a different colour, or merely not all the same.
Sections~\ref{sec:colouring-scalar} to~\ref{sec:colfive} solve it under
replica symmetry, which returns a stability line and not a colourability
threshold. Sections~\ref{sec:colouring-sp} to~\ref{sec:colouring-cq} solve it
again at one step of replica symmetry breaking, which returns both --- and
shows, with numbers of its own rather than borrowed ones, where the
replica-symmetric answer had failed.

The replica-symmetric solution is two closed forms, and they differ as far as
they can. For proper colouring the transmission of a complex does not depend on
its cardinality at all: a clique of nine members transmits exactly what a single
edge does. For the hypergraph constraint it collapses like $q^{-c}$, and by
cardinality four a complex has almost stopped constraining anything. Identical
at cardinality two, the two diverge sharply above it, and
Sec.~\ref{sec:twoconstraints-col} is why; Sec.~\ref{sec:colfive} runs both
over the five structures of Fig.~\ref{fig:mappings}, where the contrast is one
constant column beside one spanning a factor of twenty-five.

Every calculation here is carried out with the machinery of
Chapters~\ref{ch:potts} and~\ref{ch:statmech}: the transmission of
Sec.~\ref{sec:transmission}, the branching matrix of Eq.~\eqref{eq:branch},
and the one-step ansatz of Eq.~\eqref{eq:rsbansatz}. Several of the numbers
that come out are known, and the text names whose each agrees with. One is not:
the colourability threshold of a clustered graph, Sec.~\ref{sec:colouring-cq},
where the colourings run out a third earlier than on a graph while the
replica-symmetric line, in the same ensemble, does not move at all.

\section{Colouring as a hard-core problem}
\label{sec:colouringdef}

Colouring was introduced in Sec.~\ref{sec:colouring-intro}. Give every node one
of $q$ colours and forbid an edge whose endpoints agree.
There is no chemical potential and no ground-state energy to minimise: a
colouring either exists or it does not, and the question is at what density of
constraints it stops existing.

In the language of Chapter~\ref{ch:statmech} this is
Eq.~\eqref{eq:emitted} with an interior that assigns weight zero to forbidden
configurations and weight one to the rest. Nothing else changes. What does
change, before any algebra, is the \emph{message}. Up to now a message has been one number --- a probability, a field, an integer, or
at worst three states. Here it is a distribution over $q$ colours,
$\eta_{i\to a}(\sigma)$, and there is no ferromagnetic direction to project it
onto. That is the sense in which colouring is heavier than anything before it,
and Sec.~\ref{sec:colouring-scalar} is the observation that rescues it.

\paragraph{Two constraints, not one.} On a graph there is only one way to
forbid, but on a chygraph a complex of $c$ members admits two natural rules and
they are not the same.

\emph{Proper colouring} of the induced graph: every pair inside a complex is an
edge, so all $c$ members must take \emph{distinct} colours. This needs $q\ge c$
and is what one means by colouring a clustered graph whose motifs have been
promoted to complexes.

\emph{Hypergraph colouring}: only a \emph{monochromatic} complex is forbidden.
Two members may agree as long as not all of them do. This is the standard
higher-order generalisation, and it is strictly weaker.

At $c=2$ the two coincide, which is exactly why the distinction has no
counterpart on a graph. Above it they are different problems, and
Chapter~\ref{ch:cover}'s pairing of hitting set with vertex cover was the same
distinction one family earlier: how much of a complex may be left alone.

\section{What a complex transmits}
\label{sec:colouring-scalar}
\index{transmission factor}

The uniform assignment $\eta\equiv1/q$ is always a fixed point --- every colour
equally likely everywhere, which is the replica-symmetric solution. Linearise
about it and one number governs the result.

Write $\eta_{j}=1/q+w_{j}$ with $\sum_{\sigma}w_{j}(\sigma)=0$, which is forced
because both $\eta$ and $1/q$ are normalised. The perturbations therefore live
in the $(q-1)$-dimensional traceless subspace, and permuting the colours is a
symmetry of the whole problem. By Schur's lemma the linearised map acts on that
subspace as a \emph{scalar}. So although the message carries $q-1$ numbers, its
linearisation carries one, and Chapter~\ref{ch:statmech}'s branching matrix
applies unchanged with that scalar in place of $u'$. This is checked directly:
three different traceless directions return one number.

That scalar is not a new object. It is the transmission factor of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:taudef} --- the derivative of what a complex emits with respect
to what one of its members sends in, at the symmetric fixed point --- and it is
written $\tau_{c}$ here as it is there. It carries one argument rather than two
because colouring fixes the temperature. Proper colouring is the
antiferromagnetic Potts model at $\beta\to\infty$, which is $v=-1$ in
Chapter~\ref{ch:potts}'s variable, so only the number of colours is left free.

\emph{Proper colouring.} The emitted quantity is
\[
  Z(\sigma_{0})=\!\!\sum_{\substack{\sigma_{1}\dots\sigma_{c-1}\ \text{distinct}\\
    \text{and all}\ \ne\sigma_{0}}}\ \prod_{j}\eta_{j}(\sigma_{j}).
\]
Differentiate with respect to $\eta_{1}(s)$. The result is zero unless
$s\ne\sigma_{0}$, and when $s\ne\sigma_{0}$ it is the same quantity $A$ for
every such $s$, because the remaining sum depends only on how many colours have
been excluded and not on which. So the linear response is
\[
  \sum_{s}w_{1}(s)\,A\,\mathbf{1}[s\ne\sigma_{0}]
  =A\Bigl(\sum_{s}w_{1}(s)-w_{1}(\sigma_{0})\Bigr)=-A\,w_{1}(\sigma_{0}),
\]
the first sum vanishing by tracelessness. At the uniform point
$Z_{0}=q^{1-c}(q-1)!/(q-c)!$ --- one factor $1/q$ per other member, times the
number of ways to colour them distinctly and all differently from $\sigma_{0}$
--- and $A=q^{2-c}(q-2)!/(q-c)!$ by the same count with one more colour excluded
and one factor fewer, so $A/Z_{0}=q/(q-1)$.

What is emitted is $Z$ normalised, $\eta_{0}(\sigma_{0})=Z(\sigma_{0})/
\sum_{\sigma}Z(\sigma)$, and that last step is where the remaining factor comes
from. The perturbation of the numerator is traceless as well,
$\sum_{\sigma_{0}}\delta Z(\sigma_{0})=-A\sum_{\sigma_{0}}w_{1}(\sigma_{0})=0$,
so the denominator does not move at first order and stays at $qZ_{0}$. Hence
$w_{0}=\delta Z/(qZ_{0})=-(A/Z_{0})\,w_{1}/q$ --- one division by $q$ and nothing
else --- giving
\begin{equation}
  \tau_{c}(q)=-\frac{1}{q-1}.
  \label{eq:taucol}
\end{equation}
Every combinatorial factor cancels. The cardinality has gone.

Chapter~\ref{ch:potts} reaches the same value from the other side.
Equations~\eqref{eq:tau2} and~\eqref{eq:tau3} at $v=-1$ are both $-1/(q-1)$,
so the cardinality-independence is already visible in the Potts transmission at
$c=2$ and $c=3$, before this section derives it for every $c$.

\emph{Hypergraph colouring.} Here the constraint removes a single configuration,
so
\[
  Z(\sigma_{0})=1-\prod_{j}\eta_{j}(\sigma_{0}),
\]
and expanding the product to first order gives $Z_{0}=1-q^{1-c}$ and
$\delta Z(\sigma_{0})=-q^{2-c}w_{1}(\sigma_{0})$, traceless again, so the same
division by $q$ gives
\begin{equation}
  \tau^{\mathrm{hyp}}_{c}(q)=-\frac{1}{q^{\,c-1}-1}.
  \label{eq:tauhyp}
\end{equation}
At $c=2$ this is Eq.~\eqref{eq:taucol}, as it must be. \code{figs/\allowbreak colouring.\allowbreak py:tau\_\allowbreak closed} carries both closed forms, and checks them against an enumeration of the interior in rational arithmetic, so the agreement is an identity rather than a numerical coincidence.

Figure~\ref{fig:colouring} draws both: three cardinalities lie exactly on top
of one another for the proper rule, and fan out over three decades for the
hypergraph one.

\paragraph{Why the cardinality cancels, in words.} What one member
of a clique learns from another is a single fact: \emph{not that colour}. It
learns the same fact from every other member. Because the perturbation is
traceless --- the colours have to keep summing to one --- excluding one colour
is all the information there is, and excluding it again from a second member
adds nothing to the linear response. A clique of nine transmits what an edge
transmits.

The negative sign is not decoration either. Colouring is an
\emph{antiferromagnet}: raising the probability of a colour at one node lowers
it at the next. Chapter~\ref{ch:statmech} warned that the sign of the entries,
not of the leading eigenvalue, is what classifies a map, and this is the third
map in the book whose entries are negative --- after the hard-field maps of
Chapters~\ref{ch:hittingset} and~\ref{ch:cover}.

\section{The replica-symmetric threshold}
\label{sec:colouringthreshold}
\index{de Almeida--Thouless line}

Substituting into Eq.~\eqref{eq:branch} needs one decision, and it is the
decision Chapter~\ref{ch:ising} already made in Sec.~\ref{sec:atline}.

There is no ordered phase to look for here: the uniform solution \emph{is} the
replica-symmetric solution, and what one asks is whether a perturbation of
\emph{random sign} grows. That perturbation is transmitted with $\tau$ and its
effect measured by squaring, so the operative condition is the de
Almeida--Thouless form --- $\ave{u'}$ replaced by $\ave{u'^{2}}$ everywhere,
which by Eq.~\eqref{eq:reweight} is an elementwise reweighting and nothing more.
For a single layer,
\begin{equation}
  \ave{\kbar}\,(c-1)\,\tau_{c}^{2}=1.
  \label{eq:colthresh}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{The graph case, against the literature.} Put $c=2$, where a complex
is an edge and the chy-degree is the ordinary degree. What is left to choose is
the degree distribution. For a \emph{Poisson} one, $\ave{\kbar}=\ave{k}$ ---
a Poisson distribution being its own excess --- and Eq.~\eqref{eq:colthresh}
gives $\ave{k}=(q-1)^{2}$. For a \emph{regular} one, a node of degree $k$ has
$k-1$ other edges, so $\ave{\kbar}=\ave{k}-1$ and it gives
$\ave{k}=(q-1)^{2}+1$. Both are
\citeauthor{zdeborova2007}'s Eq.~(18) \citep{zdeborova2007}, which reads
$c_{\mathrm{RS}}^{\mathrm{ER}}=q^{2}-2q+1$ and
$c_{\mathrm{RS}}^{\mathrm{reg}}=q^{2}-2q+2$; the stability analysis and its
large-$q$ asymptotics are worked out by \citet{krzakala2004}, and the
replica treatment of the same ensemble by \citet{vanmourik2002}.

Two published expressions from one $\tau$, and the difference between them is
exactly the excess-degree convention this book has been carrying since
Sec.~\ref{sec:map}. That is the check the chapter needed, and it is a stronger
one than a single number would have been, because a formalism that had the
excess bookkeeping wrong would reproduce at most one of the two lines.

\paragraph{Cardinality, and the two constraints.} Now raise it. Counting in
\emph{neighbours} per node --- a complex of cardinality $c$ supplies $c-1$ of
them --- Eq.~\eqref{eq:colthresh} gives
\begin{equation}
  \text{proper: } (q-1)^{2},
  \qquad
  \text{hypergraph: } \bigl(q^{\,c-1}-1\bigr)^{2}.
  \label{eq:coltwo}
\end{equation}
The first does not move. A network of $c$-cliques loses stability at exactly the
same number of neighbours per node as a graph does, for every cardinality and
every $q$: promoting motifs to complexes changes nothing at all here. The second
runs away --- at $q=4$ it is $9$, $225$, $3969$ for $c=2,3,4$.

The second line agrees with the bound obtained by \citet{gabrie2017} for
$q$-colouring of $K$-uniform random hypergraphs: their Eq.~(43) reads
$\ell^{\mathrm{RS}}_{\mathrm{stab}}=(q^{K-1}-1)^{2}/(K-1)$ for a Poisson degree
of mean $\ell$, which is $(q^{c-1}-1)^{2}$ neighbours per node, and so
Eq.~\eqref{eq:coltwo}. Its Appendix~B derives the transmission at
finite temperature and Eq.~\eqref{eq:tauhyp} is that expression at
$\beta\to\infty$. What is left to this chapter is the first line and the
contrast: that the same substitution gives both rules, and that they part
company only above cardinality two.

The first of these is the same phenomenon as Eq.~\eqref{eq:kc}, which found
$\ave{k}(c-1)=e$ for hitting set: counted in neighbours, hyperedge size does not
move the point. Two hard-core problems, two different constants, one structure.
The reason is the same both times --- what a complex transmits and how many
neighbours it supplies move in exactly compensating ways. This is the
\emph{opposite} of the Ising model, where Sec.~\ref{sec:clustering} found the two effects failing to compensate and
the multiplicity winning.

\paragraph{Over several layers.} Equations~\eqref{eq:colthresh}
and~\eqref{eq:coltwo} are one-layer statements, and three of
Fig.~\ref{fig:mappings}'s five structures carry more than one. The
generalisation costs nothing. As in Sec.~\ref{sec:tc},
Eq.~\eqref{eq:branch} with $u'^{2}$ puts $\ave{\kbar}_{m}$ on the diagonal and
$\ave{\kappa}_{m}$ off it and multiplies both by the same
$(c_{m}-1)\tau_{m}^{2}$, so $B$ is a rank-one matrix plus a diagonal and
$\det(I-B)=0$ is a single sum:
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{l}\frac{\ave{\kappa}_{l}(c_{l}-1)\,\tau_{l}^{2}}
               {1-\bigl(\ave{\kbar}_{l}-\ave{\kappa}_{l}\bigr)(c_{l}-1)\,\tau_{l}^{2}}
  =1.
  \label{eq:colmulti}
\end{equation}
At one layer the $\ave{\kappa}$'s cancel and it is Eq.~\eqref{eq:colthresh}; at
two, expanded, it is Eq.~\eqref{eq:trifamily}, which
Sec.~\ref{sec:colouring-triangles} reaches by writing the determinant out
longhand. With Poisson layers every denominator is $1$ and the condition is
$\sum_{l}\ave{\kappa}_{l}(c_{l}-1)\tau_{l}^{2}=1$: one term per layer, and the
layers simply add.

For proper colouring that last form settles the whole question in one line.
Every $\tau_{l}$ is the same $-1/(q-1)$ whatever the cardinalities, so the sum
is $\sum_{l}\ave{\kappa}_{l}(c_{l}-1)$ times a constant --- the total
neighbours per node and nothing else. The layer list has left the problem
entirely.

\section{The two constraints diverge above cardinality two}
\label{sec:twoconstraints-col}

Equation~\eqref{eq:coltwo} says the following.

Proper colouring of a clique network is, at this level of description,
indistinguishable from colouring a graph with the same number of neighbours.
Hypergraph colouring of the same object is not remotely the same problem: a
cardinality-four complex over four colours tolerates $3969$ neighbours per node
where the proper constraint tolerates $9$.

The gap is entirely a matter of how much a complex forbids. A clique of four
rules out all but $q(q-1)(q-2)(q-3)$ of $q^{4}$ assignments; the monochromatic
rule leaves all but $q$ of them. At $c=2$ those are the same statement and the
distinction is invisible --- which is why it has no name in the graph
literature, and why it needed a formalism with cardinality in it to become
visible.

This is the third time Part~III has found a distinction that exists only above
cardinality two. Section~\ref{sec:hardfail} found the hard-field limit exact at
$c=2$ and wrong above it; Sec.~\ref{sec:corefree} found the core-free branch
present at $c=2$ and absent above it; and here two constraints coincide at $c=2$
and separate by three orders of magnitude above it. The pattern is not a
coincidence. Cardinality two is degenerate in a specific way --- a complex with
two members has no room for a partial violation --- and a great deal of what
looks like general theory is an artefact of working there.

\section{A graph with triangles}
\label{sec:colouring-triangles}

The previous section left cardinality out of the proper-colouring threshold
entirely. This section asks what that means for a clustered graph, which is
the
one place in the chapter where the book's running question --- what does
clustering do at fixed degree? --- can be put to colouring.

\paragraph{Two routes to the same object, giving different answers.} For proper
colouring a triangle-clustered graph is not a new object. A triangle forbids its
three members from agreeing pairwise, which is exactly what its three edges
already forbid, so this is ordinary $q$-colouring of an ordinary graph and the
graph formula applies to it unchanged.

Take a regular network in which every vertex sits in $\kappa$ triangles, the
triangles meeting only at vertices, so that a vertex has degree $n=2\kappa$.
Two calculations are now available for it. Read it as a chygraph of triangles
and Eq.~\eqref{eq:colthresh} gives $\ave{\kbar}(c-1)=2(\kappa-1)=n-2$. Read the
same network as a graph of degree $n$ and the excess degree is $\ave{\bar
k}=n-1$. Setting each equal to $(q-1)^{2}$,
\begin{equation}
  \text{chygraph: } n=(q-1)^{2}+2,
  \qquad
  \text{graph: } n=(q-1)^{2}+1.
  \label{eq:tridisagree}
\end{equation}
They disagree by one in degree, at every $q$.

\paragraph{The chygraph is the right one.} The graph calculation adds the
messages arriving at a vertex as though its neighbours were independent. Inside
a triangle two neighbours are adjacent: the three edges close a loop, and that
is the assumption failing. Promoting the triangle to a complex puts the loop
inside the interior sum of Eq.~\eqref{eq:emitted}, where it is summed exactly,
and what remains over triangles is treelike. So the graph answer is not an
approximation that happens to land nearby. It is short by exactly the one branch
the loop costs.

\paragraph{Both answers are one formula at its two ends.} Neither line of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:tridisagree} required dropping a layer. Take links and triangles
together at fixed degree, with $a$ the link chy-degree, $b$ the triangle
chy-degree, $n=a+2b$, and $f=2b/n$ the fraction of degree carried by triangles.
Both layers transmit the same $s=\tau^{2}=(q-1)^{-2}$, since $\tau$ does not
depend on cardinality, and the two-layer determinant of Eq.~\eqref{eq:LT}
becomes
\begin{equation}
  s\,(n-3)+2s^{2}\bigl[n(1-f/2)-1\bigr]=1.
  \label{eq:trifamily}
\end{equation}
At $f=0$ it returns $(q-1)^{2}+1$; at $f=1$ it returns $(q-1)^{2}+2$; both
exactly, in rational arithmetic. The cross term does all of the interpolating.

\paragraph{How large is the correction.} One in degree, and it is worth putting
that as a fraction of the threshold it corrects, $1/[(q-1)^{2}+2]$: $17$ per
cent at $q=3$, $9$ at $q=4$, $5.6$ at $q=5$, and about $1$ by $q=10$. The graph
calculation is badly wrong when there are few colours and nearly right when
there are many, which is what one should expect --- the branch it loses is one
of $n$, and $n$ grows like $q^{2}$.

\paragraph{Where the correction vanishes.} For Poisson layers there is none.
There $\ave{\kbar}=\ave{\kappa}$: a Poisson graph is its own excess and so is a
Poisson triangle layer, so the cross term
$\ave{\kappa}_{|}\ave{\kappa}_{\triangle}-\ave{\kbar}_{|}\ave{\kbar}_{\triangle}$
vanishes identically, Eq.~\eqref{eq:trifamily} returns $(q-1)^{2}$ at every $f$,
and the graph calculation gives the right answer. It does so by accident. Its
independence assumption is exactly as false as before; what saves it is that the
quantity it miscounts --- the excess-degree correction --- is one a Poisson
degree distribution does not carry.

So this is not a statement that clustering moves the colouring threshold. What
moves is the excess-degree bookkeeping: a regular vertex reached through a
triangle has one fewer triangle to pass the message on to, and that lost branch
is the entire effect. It is the same lost branch that separates
$(q-1)^{2}$ from $(q-1)^{2}+1$ in Sec.~\ref{sec:colouringthreshold}, arriving
somewhere it is easy to mistake for physics.

\paragraph{Why only one branch.} Because proper colouring cannot see
cardinality. A triangle transmits precisely what an edge transmits,
Sec.~\ref{sec:colouring-scalar}, so absorbing its loop into a complex changes
how many branches leave a vertex and leaves what travels along them alone. Only
the count is corrected, and a count is worth one.

Where the constraint \emph{does} see cardinality the same promotion is worth
much more. For the hypergraph rule of Sec.~\ref{sec:twoconstraints-col} the
threshold runs $9$, $225$, $3969$ at cardinalities $2$, $3$, $4$ with $q=4$:
three orders of magnitude, from the identical operation on the identical
structure. Promoting a motif to a complex is always the right treatment of its
loop. What that treatment is worth in numbers is decided by the interaction
inside the complex, and proper colouring is the case where it is worth least.

\section{Structured networks}
\label{sec:colfive}

Chapter~\ref{ch:chygraphs} claimed that one calculation covers the five
structures of Fig.~\ref{fig:mappings}. Sections~\ref{sec:isingfive},
\ref{sec:hsfive} and~\ref{sec:fivecover} pay that out for the critical
temperature, for hitting set and for vertex cover. Here it is paid out twice,
once per interior rule, and the two answers are as far apart as they can be:
one column of Table~\ref{tab:fivecol} is constant down its whole length and the
other spans a factor of twenty-five. Same five structures, same
Eq.~\eqref{eq:colmulti}, and the only thing changed between the columns is what
a complex forbids.

\paragraph{A graph} is one layer of links. One term in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:colmulti} gives $\ave{\kbar}\tau^{2}=1$, which is
Eq.~\eqref{eq:colthresh}, and with $\tau=-1/(q-1)$ the stability line is
\begin{equation}
  \ave{\kbar}=(q-1)^{2},
  \label{eq:colgraphline}
\end{equation}
neighbours per node on the left and colours on the right. The two rules
coincide at cardinality two, so this is the whole row. For example, at $q=4$ that is $9$ neighbours per node for a
Poisson degree and $10$ for a regular one. Both are
\citeauthor{zdeborova2007}'s, and Sec.~\ref{sec:colouringthreshold} has already
used the pair as the chapter's check.

\paragraph{A hypergraph} is one complex layer with the cardinality free, and it
is where the two rules separate. Take it at a single size $c$. The single term is $\ave{\kbar}(c-1)\tau_{c}^{2}=1$. In
neighbours per node the proper rule gives $(q-1)^{2}$ with a Poisson degree,
since $\tau$ does not depend on $c$, and $(q-1)^{2}+(c-1)$ with a regular one
--- cardinality reaching the answer only through the excess convention, and
only by $c-1$ in degree, which is Eq.~\eqref{eq:tridisagree}'s pair of lines at
$c=2$ and $c=3$. The hypergraph rule gives $(q^{\,c-1}-1)^{2}$. That is
Eq.~\eqref{eq:coltwo}. For example, at $q=4$ and $c=3$ --- three-cliques under
the proper rule, $3$-hyperedges under the weaker one --- the two give $9$ and
$225$. The separation is the whole content of
Sec.~\ref{sec:twoconstraints-col} read structure by structure. Under the proper rule it is still $9$: a triangle
forbids what its three edges forbid, transmits what an edge transmits, and
supplies twice the neighbours at half the chy-degree, so nothing moves. Under
the hypergraph rule it is $225$. The object on the page is identical in the two
cases. What differs is whether all three members must disagree or merely not all
three agree, and that one word is worth a factor of twenty-five.

\paragraph{A multiplex network} is $L$ link layers on the same vertices. Every
layer is at $c=2$, where the two rules coincide and $\tau$ is the same in every
layer, so Eq.~\eqref{eq:colmulti} reads
\[
  \sum_{l}\frac{\ave{\kappa}_{l}\tau^{2}}
                {1-\bigl(\ave{\kbar}_{l}-\ave{\kappa}_{l}\bigr)\tau^{2}}=1,
\]
and only the total survives: $(q-1)^{2}$ neighbours per node with Poisson
layers, $(q-1)^{2}+1$ with regular ones, whatever $L$ is. For example, two link
layers sharing the degree evenly give $9$ at $q=4$, the graph's number under
both rules. A multiplex is a graph carrying labels that proper colouring
cannot read.

\paragraph{Interacting graphs and hypergraphs} is $L$ layers whose
cardinalities need not agree. Under the proper rule that costs almost nothing,
every $\tau_{l}$ being $-1/(q-1)$ regardless: for $L$ layers of one cardinality
$c$ the line is $(q-1)^{2}$ neighbours per node with Poisson layers and
$(q-1)^{2}+(c-1)$ with regular ones, the same pair the single layer gives and
independent of $L$. Under the hypergraph rule each layer carries its own
$\tau_{c_{l}}$ and the terms no longer share a factor. For example, three link
layers sharing the degree equally give $9$, and three layers of cardinality
three give $225$ under the weaker rule and $9$ under the proper one. The panel
follows its layer list and adds nothing else. As in
Chapter~\ref{ch:ising} and unlike Chapters~\ref{ch:hittingset}
and~\ref{ch:cover}, the cross layer is not a special case: it contributes its
term and the sum is all there is.

\paragraph{A clustered graph} is a link layer together with one layer per clique
size present, here a link layer and a triangle layer. Under the proper rule
every layer carries the same $\tau$, so with Poisson
layers the two terms still add to the total neighbours per node and nothing
moves; with regular layers the denominators of Eq.~\eqref{eq:colmulti} differ
between the layers, $1+\tau^{2}$ against $1+2\tau^{2}$, and the answer lies
strictly between the two pure cases. For example, half the degree in each gives
$10.476$ --- which is Eq.~\eqref{eq:trifamily} at $f=1/2$, exactly, and lies
between the $10$ of all links and the $11$ of all triangles as that equation
says it must. Under the Poisson convention it is $9$ like everything else. The
row is Sec.~\ref{sec:colouring-triangles}'s result appearing as one entry in a
list, and the interval it lies in is one in degree wide.

\paragraph{The column that is constant, and what it does not say.} It does not
say that structure is irrelevant to colouring. It says that this
\emph{line} cannot see it. Section~\ref{sec:colouring-sp-graph} puts the
line on the wrong side of the colourability threshold from $q=4$ upward, and
Sec.~\ref{sec:colouring-cq} measures what the constant column misses: promoting
links into triangles at fixed degree lowers the colourability threshold
$\kbar_{q}$ of Sec.~\ref{sec:colouring-sp} by a third, from $4.68$ to $3.07$ at
$q=3$, while the proper column here does not move by so much as a
digit. A reader who took the constant column as a statement about colouring
would have the sign of the largest effect in the chapter replaced by a zero.
The table is a statement about local stability, and the chapter says from
Sec.~\ref{sec:colouring-sp} onwards that local stability is not the
question.

\paragraph{What the list establishes.} That the five structures are one
substitution, which is Chapter~\ref{ch:chygraphs}'s claim, and that what the
substitution is worth depends entirely on the interior. Nothing in
Table~\ref{tab:fivecol} varies between the columns except what a complex
forbids, and that alone moves the answer from a constant to a factor of
twenty-five. Promoting a motif to a complex is always the right treatment of
its loop; proper colouring is the case where the right treatment is worth
nothing, and the hypergraph rule on the same six rows is the case where it is
worth everything.

The replica-symmetric solution assumes that the colourings form a single
cluster. Within that assumption it says where the solution is stable and where
it is not, and that is Eq.~\eqref{eq:colthresh}:
$\ave{\kbar}(c-1)\tau_{c}^{2}=1$, or
\[
  \kbar_{s}=\frac{1}{(c-1)\,\tau_{c}^{2}}.
\]
For $\ave{\kbar}<\kbar_{s}$ a perturbation of random sign decays and the
solution is locally stable; above it the perturbation grows and the solution is
not. On a graph under the proper rule this is Eq.~\eqref{eq:colgraphline},
$\kbar_{s}=(q-1)^{2}$, which is the column
Table~\ref{tab:colpublished} carries.

But the assumption can fail while the solution is still stable. The colourings
may already be fragmented into many clusters over the whole of that region, or
part of it, and no calculation that presumes a single cluster can see it.
Settling that takes one step of replica symmetry breaking.

What is needed is Sec.~\ref{sec:onestep} in its $m=0$ form: surveys travelling
along the inclusions in place of fields, and the complexity $\Sigma$ of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:sigmagen} counting the clusters they describe.
Figure~\ref{fig:survey} is what a survey is, and what changes when the
solutions shatter. It returns two densities where replica symmetry returned
one:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}
\item $\kbar_{d}(q)$, the \emph{clustering transition}: below it the
      colourings form a single cluster, above it they shatter into many;
\item $\kbar_{q}(q)$, the \emph{colourability threshold}: below it proper
      colourings exist, above it none do.
\end{itemize}
\section{One-step replica symmetry breaking, graphs}
\label{sec:colouring-sp-graph}
\index{survey propagation}

Start with graph colouring, borrowing the results of \citet{mulet2002} and the
algorithm of \citet{braunstein2003}.

Here a survey is a distribution, over the clusters of colourings, of the colour
a node is forced to, and colour symmetry does most of the work of carrying it. A survey is uniform over colours, so one scalar per
inclusion suffices --- $e$, the probability that a node is forced at all, with
each colour taking $e/q$ of it.

What symmetry does \emph{not} do is decouple the colours, and this is the whole
of the calculation. A forced neighbour forbids exactly one colour, so ``every
colour but one is forbidden'' is a question about whether $q-1$ colours are
\emph{covered} by the forced neighbours, not a product of $q-1$ independent
events. Inclusion--exclusion over which colours are missed gives
\begin{equation}
  \begin{split}
    P(\text{only }c\text{ free})&=\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}(-1)^{j}\binom{q-1}{j}
      \prod_{k}\Bigl(1-\frac{e_{k}(1+j)}{q}\Bigr),\\
    P(\text{none free})&=\sum_{j=0}^{q}(-1)^{j}\binom{q}{j}
      \prod_{k}\Bigl(1-\frac{e_{k}j}{q}\Bigr),
  \end{split}
  \label{eq:colsp}
\end{equation}
the product running over the other neighbours, and the survey update being the
first divided by one minus the second --- the contradictory state discarded,
which is the one thing the $m=0$ reweighting of Sec.~\ref{sec:onestep} does. Treating the colours as independent instead is the
obvious shortcut and it is wrong; it is caught by asking for three thresholds
rather than one.

The complexity is Eq.~\eqref{eq:sigmagen}, whose three terms collapse to two
here: a graph has one layer, and its complex and inclusion terms combine into a
single weight per edge,
\begin{equation}
  \Sigma=\bigl\langle\ln\bigl(1-P(\text{none free})\bigr)\bigr\rangle
        -\frac{\ave{k}}{2}\Bigl\langle\ln\Bigl(1-\frac{e_{i}e_{j}}{q}\Bigr)
        \Bigr\rangle,
  \label{eq:colsigma}
\end{equation}
the edge term being the weight of two ends not forced to the same colour.

\section{One-step replica symmetry breaking, hypergraphs}
\label{sec:colouring-sp-chy}
\index{survey propagation}

In the hard-field limit the
message from a complex is the set of colours it forbids the member it is
speaking to. Everything about how that message combines with the others at a
node turns on one question: \emph{how many colours can a single complex forbid
at once?}

For the hypergraph rule the answer is \textbf{at most one}. A complex is
violated only when all of its members share a colour, so it can forbid $\gamma$
to member $0$ only when every one of the other $c-1$ members is already forced
to $\gamma$ --- and they cannot all be forced to two different colours at the
same time. The message is therefore the same \emph{kind} of object as at
cardinality two, a single forbidden colour or none, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:colsp}
carries over with one substitution: the weight $e/q$ that a neighbour forbids a
given colour becomes $h/q$, where
\begin{equation}
  h=q\prod_{j=1}^{c-1}\frac{e_{j}}{q}
  \label{eq:hypsp}
\end{equation}
is the weight that the complex forbids anything at all. Nothing else in the
node update changes, and the inclusion--exclusion of
Sec.~\ref{sec:colouring-sp-graph} is reused unaltered.

For the proper rule the answer is \textbf{up to $c-1$}. Every member forced to a
different colour removes a different colour from member $0$, so a complex of
cardinality five can arrive carrying four prohibitions at once. The message is
no longer a colour but a \emph{set} of them, the events ``$\gamma$ is
forbidden'' and ``$\gamma'$ is forbidden'' are correlated within a single
complex as well as across complexes, and the node update needs the joint
distribution of the forbidden set rather than a scalar. Equation~\eqref{eq:colsp}
does not carry over, and no substitution repairs it.

That asymmetry is Sec.~\ref{sec:twoconstraints-col} again, one level up. There
the two rules were found to coincide at cardinality two and to separate above
it, in what they transmit. Here they coincide at cardinality two and separate
above it in something harder: whether the one-step machinery can be reused at
all. A complex with two members has no room for a partial violation, and it also
has no room to forbid two things at once; both statements are the same
degeneracy, and it is the second that decides how much work the next calculation
is.

\paragraph{Carrying it out.} Put Eq.~\eqref{eq:hypsp} into
Eq.~\eqref{eq:colsp}. The complexity of Eq.~\eqref{eq:colsigma} gains the one
term a graph does not have: a complex is violated when all $c$ of its members
agree, so it contributes
$\ln\bigl[1-q\prod_{j\le c}(e_{j}/q)\bigr]$, and there are
$\ave{k}/c$ complexes per node.

\section{One-step replica symmetry breaking, clustered graphs}
\label{sec:colouring-cq}
\index{survey propagation}

This section solves the problem on a clustered graph: a graph whose links have
been promoted into triangles, the construction of
Sec.~\ref{sec:colouring-triangles}, coloured under the proper rule. It is the
one calculation in the chapter whose answer is not already known.

Both ensembles here are Poisson, and are written $\mathrm{Poisson}$ and
$\mathrm{Poisson}_{\triangle}$. The first is a Poisson graph of mean degree
$\ave{k}$. In the second an atom sits in a Poisson number of triangles of mean
$\ave{\kappa}$, and each triangle gives it two neighbours. The two are compared
at the same $\ave{k}$, setting $\ave{\kappa}=\ave{k}/2$.
Section~\ref{sec:colouring-triangles} made the same promotion in a
\emph{regular} ensemble, where a vertex sits in exactly $\kappa$ triangles. The
two are not interchangeable: they give opposite readings of the stability line,
which is where this section ends.

The new difficulty is what a message has to carry. For the hypergraph rule a
complex forbids at most one colour, so the message stayed the kind of object it
was at cardinality two and Eq.~\eqref{eq:hypsp} was the whole of the
difference. For the proper rule a complex forbids up to $c-1$ colours at once.
The message is then a set of colours rather than one colour, and there is no
scalar to substitute.

So the survey has to get heavier. In place of the single $e$ of
Sec.~\ref{sec:colouring-sp-graph} it carries one number for each subset of colours a
cluster might leave open to a member, and the interior of a complex is summed
over those subsets. That is the last substitution in the chapter and the only
one that costs anything.

\paragraph{The obstacle.} A survey carrying one forbidden colour cannot carry
the $c-1$ a complex closes at once, and no scalar substitution repairs it. Nor
does the graph calculation: \citet{krzakala2004} locate $\kbar_{q}$ on a graph with
a survey update over edges and a complexity that is a site term over the degree
distribution plus a term per link, and neither expression has anywhere to put a
complex.

\paragraph{The calculation.} It comes from Sec.~\ref{sec:onestep} instead.
Equation~\eqref{eq:rsbansatz} is the one-step ansatz for any model on a
chygraph, written before a model is named, and nothing in it has to be guessed
one level up, because Eq.~\eqref{eq:Fchy} was never written for two-body
interactions in the first place. Everything below is
Eq.~\eqref{eq:rsbansatz} with one thing named: $\nu_{h}$, the one-site law a
message stands for. Here a message is the set $T\subseteq\{1,\dots,q\}$ of
colours a cluster leaves open to a member and $\nu_{T}$ is the uniform law on
$T$, so the survey $Q$ is a distribution over the $2^{q}$ subsets and
$\mathcal{P}_{l}[Q]$ is what the population carries. Two things follow. The
interior sum is Eq.~\eqref{eq:Xichy} on that alphabet: given the sets the other
$c-1$ members leave open, what is left open to the member entered by is what
Hall's condition permits, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:colsp}'s inclusion--exclusion is
that count. And the complexity is Eq.~\eqref{eq:sigmagen} on it --- one term
per atom, $\ave{\kappa}/c$ per complex, $\ave{\kappa}$ subtracted per
inclusion --- which is Eq.~\eqref{eq:colsigma}.

The subsets do not all have to be carried. Colouring is symmetric under
permuting the colours, so a survey depends on a subset only through its size,
and the population stores $q+1$ numbers per sample rather than $2^{q}$. What
the node update needs is the chance that an emitted set contains a given
$t$-subset, a hypergeometric weight over the sizes; that reduction, and not the
ansatz, is what makes the calculation run.

\paragraph{Two gates before it is used.} At $c=2$, writing $\eta=e/q$, the
update matches \citeauthor{krzakala2004}'s term for term, and
Eq.~\eqref{eq:colsigma} becomes their site sum together with
$-\tfrac{\ave{k}}{2}\ln(1-q\eta_{1}\eta_{2})$, which is why
Sec.~\ref{sec:colouring-sp-graph} returns \citeauthor{mulet2002}'s $\kbar_{q}$ to a
tenth of a per cent. The second gate is \citeauthor{gabrie2017} at $c=3$ under
the hypergraph rule, which exercises the cardinality-three scaffolding with the
interior swapped and nothing else. Only then is it turned on triangles.

\paragraph{Where the colourable phase ends.} $\Sigma(y=\infty)$ counts
zero-energy clusters --- clusters of proper colourings --- so the phase ends
where it stops being positive. On a graph that happens at a crossing: the survey
is trivial, a branch appears at $\kbar_{d}$ with $\Sigma>0$, and the arc it makes
falls through zero. On a network of triangles the branch appears with $\Sigma$
already negative, the arc is absent, and the phase ends where the branch does.
Figures~\ref{fig:threshold} and~\ref{fig:phases} draw the two cases: a graph
passes through one cluster, then exponentially many, then none, while triangles
go from one cluster to none with the shattered phase skipped.

\paragraph{Which end of that is computed.} Not both, and the asymmetry decides
how the triangle number should be read. \emph{Above} the lift-off there is a
solution and it carries $\Sigma<0$: fewer than one cluster of proper colourings,
so the colourings are gone. That is a result. \emph{Below} it there is no
solution but the trivial one, and the $\Sigma=0$ recorded there is the
replica-symmetric default --- one cluster because nothing has shattered --- and
not a count of anything.

So what the calculation delivers is a bound from above. Proper colourings have
certainly run out by degree $3.07$ at $q=3$; that they survive all the way to it
is the assumption that the shattered phase has zero width rather than a width
too narrow to resolve at this population. On a graph the two ends are pinned
together by the arc, which is continuous and crosses zero once, and $\kbar_{q}$ is
bracketed from both sides. A fold offers no such argument, and losing the window
costs exactly that. What it does not cost is the conclusion, which needs the
bound alone: $3.07$ against a graph's $4.69$ says the colourings have gone, and
a true threshold lower still would only widen the gap.

\paragraph{That assumption, tested.} It has two halves and they fail together
or not at all. Whether the window is too narrow to resolve is a question about
the population: the onset moves from degree $3.078$ to $3.073$ between $4\,000$
samples and $60\,000$, with the spread over seeds falling from $0.004$ to
$0.001$, so the fold is converged rather than resolution-limited. Whether a
shattered solution hides below it is a question about the reweighting, and at
zero temperature that reweighting is not a free choice: $y$ is the Parisi
parameter rescaled, $y=\beta m$ with $\beta\to\infty$, so a scan in $y$
\emph{is} the finite-$m$ calculation rather than a substitute for one.
Softening the constraint --- a member left with no colour surviving at weight
$e^{-y}$ instead of being discarded --- moves the onset by less than $10^{-3}$
in degree between $y=0.5$ and $y=\infty$, from a population started
\emph{in} the shattered state, so that below the fold it collapses rather than
failing to be found. And $\Sigma$ at the lift-off is $-0.10$, falling to
$-0.20$ by degree $3.4$: it does not approach zero from below.

So the window is absent and not unresolved, and the bound is a bound for that
reason rather than for want of looking. One thing this does not reach is
condensation, which sorts clusters by size where $y$ sorts them by energy, and
which needs the $m=1$ object Sec.~\ref{sec:onestep} does not carry;
Sec.~\ref{sec:notdone} keeps it.

\paragraph{Triangles make colouring harder.} Table~\ref{tab:cq} is the result.
A triangle-clustered graph has stopped being $3$-colourable by degree $3.07$
where a triangle-free one survives to $4.68$, and stopped being $4$-colourable
by $6.75$ against $8.90$: a third and a quarter of the range, gone.

Set that beside Sec.~\ref{sec:colouring-triangles}, and be careful about which
ensemble is being quoted, because the stability line does two different things
and neither of them is this. In the Poisson ensemble --- the one both columns
above are computed in --- it does not move at all: the cross term vanishes and
Eq.~\eqref{eq:trifamily} returns $(q-1)^{2}$ at every triangle fraction. In the
regular ensemble it does move, and it moves the wrong way, from $(q-1)^{2}+1$ to
$(q-1)^{2}+2$.

Both are correct, and both are a different line from $\kbar_{q}$ --- one is where a
solution stops being locally stable, the other where solutions stop existing ---
but the consequence is worth stating flatly. On a graph the two at least land
near one another --- $4$ against $4.69$, $9$ against $8.90$, $16$ against
$13.69$ --- which is why the stability line gets used as a rough locator at all,
even after Sec.~\ref{sec:colouring-sp-graph} has put it on the wrong side from $q=4$.
On triangles it is not near, and it is not even wrong in a fixed direction:
taken as a proxy, the Poisson stability line says that clustering does nothing
to colouring and the regular one says that it makes colouring \emph{easier}. It
makes it harder, by a third.

\paragraph{What is not computed.} The energy. Above $\kbar_{q}$ some fraction of
complexes must be violated, and $\Sigma$ says nothing about how large that
fraction is. It is \citeauthor{krzakala2004}'s $e(y)$, which needs the
reweighted free energy at finite $y$ rather than the $y\to\infty$ endpoint used
here. What is settled is where the colourings run out, not how badly.

\section{Checks}
\label{sec:colouring-checks}

\checkhead{Known results}
Equation~\eqref{eq:colthresh} at $c=2$ returns $(q-1)^{2}$ for a Poisson degree
distribution and $(q-1)^{2}+1$ for a regular one, which are the two lines of
\citeauthor{zdeborova2007}'s Eq.~(18), and Eq.~\eqref{eq:coltwo}'s hypergraph
line is \citeauthor{gabrie2017}'s Eq.~(43). Section~\ref{sec:colouring-sp-graph}
returns \citeauthor{mulet2002}'s $\kbar_{q}$ at cardinality two for $q=3,4,5$ to a
tenth of a per cent, and the clustering threshold $\kbar_{d}$ out of the same
population as the density at which the survey stops being trivial.
Section~\ref{sec:colouring-sp-chy} returns \citeauthor{gabrie2017}'s hypergraph
thresholds at $(q,c)=(3,3)$, $(4,3)$ and $(2,5)$, with the two cases that
reference marks invalid excluded rather than reported. Reproducing these
constants checks the implementation; none of them is established here.

\checkhead{New results}
The triangle column of Table~\ref{tab:cq}, and nothing else in the chapter.
Equations~\eqref{eq:taucol} and~\eqref{eq:tauhyp} behind it are checked against a
direct enumeration of the interior of a complex, in rational arithmetic so that
the agreement is exact rather than numerical: proper colouring for
$q=3,\dots,7$ at every cardinality from $2$ to $\min(q,5)$, and hypergraph
colouring for $q=3,4,5$ at cardinalities $2$, $3$ and $4$. The claim that the
linearised map acts as a scalar on the traceless subspace is checked by
perturbing along three different traceless directions and requiring the same
answer, for both rules. Equation~\eqref{eq:colmulti} reproduces
Eq.~\eqref{eq:colthresh} at one layer and Eq.~\eqref{eq:trifamily} at two ---
the latter exactly, in rational arithmetic, which is what
Table~\ref{tab:fivecol}'s clustered row is --- and the table's proper column is
checked to be $(q-1)^{2}$ on every row. And the set-valued machinery Sec.~\ref{sec:colouring-cq}
needs is gated against the two known cases before it is used ---
\citeauthor{mulet2002} at $c=2$, \citeauthor{gabrie2017} at $c=3$ --- so what is
new when it runs on triangles is the interior and not the apparatus.

\checkhead{Partial results}
The triangle $\kbar_{q}$ is a bound from above rather than a located threshold. On a
graph the complexity arcs above zero and crosses it once, so $\kbar_{q}$ is
bracketed from both sides; on triangles $\Sigma$ is negative wherever the
shattered branch exists, so the threshold is where that branch appears --- a fold
rather than a crossing, and the worse-determined of the two, as the $\kbar_{d}$
figures already show. What makes it a bound is checked rather than assumed:
the onset is converged in the population, it does not move with the
reweighting $y$ --- which at zero temperature is the Parisi parameter and not
a separate knob --- and $\Sigma$ at the lift-off is $-0.10$ rather than nearly
zero, so the shattered window is absent and not unresolved. What is not
reached is condensation. What is checked is the statement the number is used
for:
that it lies below the graph's $\kbar_{q}$ and below the stability line. Both are
inequalities, so the direction --- clustering makes colouring harder --- does not
depend on the third figure or on the bound being tight. Two further limits:
Eq.~\eqref{eq:colthresh} is a local stability line and not a colourability
threshold, which is what Sec.~\ref{sec:colouring-sp} is about; and the one-step
calculation is at $m=0$, so the condensation and rigidity transitions are
outside it.
