\chapter{Localization on a clustered graph}
\label{ch:localization}

% Added 2026-09-25.  Numbers from statmech/probe/anderson.py (scan); figure and
% tables from figs/anderson.py.  See book/extensions.md.

Every message so far has been a probability, a field, a survey or a count.
This chapter carries the recursion with a message that is a Green's
function, on the problem that has been the Bethe lattice's since
\citet{abouchacra1973}: a quantum particle hopping on a graph with random
site energies, which is localised or extended depending on energy and
disorder \citep{anderson1958}. The occasion is a specific one.
\citet{tonetti2025} test, on the $K+1=3$ Bethe lattice, the proposal of
\citet{filoche2012} that the mobility edge is the percolation threshold of
a classical landscape computed from the same Hamiltonian, and find that the
two lines do not coincide and are of different universality classes. They
attribute the discrepancy to interference, which the landscape cannot see,
and note that the agreement they and others find in three dimensions
deteriorates with dimension and on tree-like structures --- which makes the
tree the worst case and suggests that loops should help. A chygraph with
triangles has the shortest loops, and treats them exactly. So the question
this chapter asks is the one their paper leaves: do loops bring the two
lines together. They do not; they part them further.

\section{The Anderson model on a line}
\label{sec:line}
\index{Anderson model|textbf}
\index{Green's function|textbf}
\index{local density of states|textbf}

The Anderson model is one quantum particle on the vertices of a graph,
\begin{equation}
  \hat H=\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i}\,|i\rangle\langle i|
  -t\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}\bigl(|i\rangle\langle j|+|j\rangle\langle i|\bigr).
  \label{eq:anderson}
\end{equation}
The first term gives site $i$ an energy $\epsilon_{i}$, drawn
independently and uniformly on $[-W/2,W/2]$, so that $W$ measures the
disorder; the second lets the particle hop between neighbouring sites
with amplitude $t$, set to $1$ throughout. A stationary state of energy
$E$ is an amplitude $\psi(i)$ on every site, and the question the model
asks is what those amplitudes look like. Without disorder every site is
alike, each stationary state spreads its amplitude over the whole graph
as a plane wave does on a lattice, and a particle put on one site spreads
away from it. A weak disorder scatters those states without pinning them,
and the spreading becomes diffusive. A strong one pins each state to a
few sites, with an amplitude that decays exponentially away from its
centre, and a particle put on a site stays near it for ever
\citep{anderson1958}. States of the first kind are called extended and of
the second localised. In between, above two dimensions and on the tree,
the spectrum holds both at once, sorted by energy: the states in the
middle of the band of allowed energies are extended, and those in its
tails, where a state exists only on an unlikely run of site energies, are
localised. The energy that separates the two is the mobility edge
$E_{c}(W)$. It moves inwards as $W$ grows, until the two edges meet at
the band centre at a critical disorder $W_{c}$ beyond which every state
is localised. Diffusion, conductance and the statistics of the levels all
change across this one line, which is why the chapter is about where it
lies.

The line is the place to meet the objects the chapter computes, because
on it everything can be done twice, once from the stationary states and
once without them. Take a chain of $N$ sites with periodic boundaries and
no disorder, $\epsilon_{j}=0$. Every site is alike, so the stationary
states are plane waves, $\psi_{k}(j)=e^{\mathrm{i}kj}/\sqrt{N}$ with
$k=2\pi n/N$, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:anderson} acting on one gives
\begin{equation}
  E(k)=-2t\cos k,
  \label{eq:linedispersion}
\end{equation}
so the allowed energies fill the band $|E|\le2t$, and each state puts the
same weight $1/N$ on every site: extended, in the sense of the opening
paragraph. How many states there are per unit energy per site is the
density of states,
\begin{equation}
  \begin{split}
  \rho(E)&=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k}\delta\bigl(E-E(k)\bigr)
  =\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{dk}{2\pi}\,\delta(E+2t\cos k)\\
  &=\frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{4t^{2}-E^{2}}},
  \qquad |E|<2t,
  \end{split}
  \label{eq:linedos}
\end{equation}
which is zero outside the band and diverges at its edges, where $E(k)$
flattens and the states pile up.

The resolvent packs the same information into one function, and it is
the function behind the experiment of the opening paragraph.
$\mathcal{G}(z)=(\hat H-z)^{-1}$ is defined off the real axis, at
$z=E+\mathrm{i}\eta$ with $\eta>0$. Put the particle on site $j$ at time
zero: the amplitude to find it there at time $t$ is
$\langle j|e^{-\mathrm{i}\hat Ht}|j\rangle$, with $\hbar=1$, and the
diagonal element of the resolvent is that return amplitude transformed
in time,
$\mathcal{G}_{jj}(z)=\mathrm{i}\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,e^{\mathrm{i}zt}
\langle j|e^{-\mathrm{i}\hat Ht}|j\rangle$. The energy $E$ selects a
frequency, and the factor $e^{-\eta t}$ cuts the integral off at times
of order $1/\eta$, so $\mathcal{G}_{jj}(E+\mathrm{i}\eta)$ is the
particle's return to $j$ at energy $E$, watched for a time $1/\eta$. A
particle that spreads away has a return amplitude that decays and one
that is pinned has a return amplitude that does not, and the resolvent
turns that difference into a function of energy. In the basis of
stationary states the diagonal element is
\begin{equation}
  \begin{split}
  \mathcal{G}_{jj}(z)&=\sum_{n}\frac{|\psi_{n}(j)|^{2}}{E_{n}-z},\\
  \frac{1}{\pi}\operatorname{Im}\mathcal{G}_{jj}(E+\mathrm{i}0^{+})
  &=\sum_{n}|\psi_{n}(j)|^{2}\,\delta(E-E_{n}),
  \end{split}
  \label{eq:ldos}
\end{equation}
the second by $\operatorname{Im}1/(x-\mathrm{i}\eta)\to\pi\delta(x)$. The
right-hand side is the local density of states at $j$: the density of
states, weighted by how much of each state sits on that site. On the
clean line the weight is $1/N$ for every state, so the local density is
Eq.~\eqref{eq:linedos} at every site, and the sum over $k$ can be done
directly,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{G}_{jj}(z)=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{dk}{2\pi}\,
  \frac{1}{-2t\cos k-z}
  =-\frac{1}{\sqrt{z^{2}-4t^{2}}},
  \label{eq:lineG}
\end{equation}
on the branch with $\operatorname{Im}\mathcal{G}>0$ for
$\operatorname{Im}z>0$. At $z=E+\mathrm{i}0^{+}$ inside the band the square
root is imaginary and $\operatorname{Im}\mathcal{G}_{jj}/\pi$ is
Eq.~\eqref{eq:linedos}; outside the band it is real and the imaginary
part is zero.

Now the second way, which needs no stationary states and survives
disorder. Remove site $j$ from the chain. What is left is two half-lines,
and the resolvent of the chain at $j$ is that of the isolated site,
$1/(\epsilon_{j}-z)$, corrected by the hops out into each half-line and
back,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{G}_{jj}^{-1}=\epsilon_{j}-z
  -t^{2}\mathcal{G}_{j+1\to j}-t^{2}\mathcal{G}_{j-1\to j},
  \label{eq:lineschur}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{G}_{j+1\to j}$ is the diagonal element, at its end site,
of the half-line that starts at $j+1$ with $j$ removed. This is the corner
element of the inverse of a bordered matrix, and it will return in
Sec.~\ref{sec:resolvent} as a Schur complement. The end element of a
half-line obeys the same rule with one neighbour instead of two, because
a half-line with its end removed is a half-line again,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{G}_{j+1\to j}^{-1}=\epsilon_{j+1}-z-t^{2}\mathcal{G}_{j+2\to j+1},
  \label{eq:linecavity}
\end{equation}
a recursion along the line, and an exact one. Without disorder every
half-line is the same, so its end element is one number $g$,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:linecavity} is the quadratic $g^{-1}=-z-t^{2}g$, and
\begin{equation}
  g(z)=\frac{-z+\sqrt{z^{2}-4t^{2}}}{2t^{2}},
  \qquad
  \mathcal{G}_{jj}^{-1}=-z-2t^{2}g=-\sqrt{z^{2}-4t^{2}},
  \label{eq:lineg}
\end{equation}
which is Eq.~\eqref{eq:lineG} again. The band, the density of states and
the extended character of every state have come out of a recursion that
never mentioned a plane wave.

That is the object the chapter computes, and the way it computes it.
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:lineschur} and~\eqref{eq:linecavity} are written for
arbitrary $\epsilon_{j}$: with disorder the plane waves are gone and there
is no closed form, but the recursion runs as before, one site at a time,
and each $\mathcal{G}_{jj}$ is still built from the site's own energy and
the two half-lines beside it. Eq.~\eqref{eq:ldos} says what to read off
it. In the extended phase infinitely many states contribute to the local
density at $j$, each with an infinitesimal weight, and
$\operatorname{Im}\mathcal{G}_{jj}(E+\mathrm{i}0^{+})$ is a smooth function
of $E$, as on the clean line. In the localised phase the states that
reach site $j$ are a discrete set, the local density is a comb of
$\delta$ peaks at their energies, and the imaginary part vanishes at
almost every $E$. So the phase is decided site by site by whether
$\operatorname{Im}\mathcal{G}_{jj}(E+\mathrm{i}0^{+})$ is finite or zero, a
single number per site, which is what a message can carry.

The line shows only one of the two phases at finite disorder. On it every
state is localised at any $W>0$, as \citet{mott1961} first argued from a
chain of tall barriers and \citet{ishii1973} showed in general, so the
extended phase exists only at $W=0$ and there is no mobility edge to
find: two half-lines
are too few branches for an imaginary part to survive the scattering. The
tree gives each site $K+1$ branches, and there the same recursion has an
extended phase at finite disorder, a mobility edge, and everything the
opening paragraph described.

\section{The resolvent as a message}
\label{sec:resolvent}
\index{Schur complement|textbf}

On a tree, removing site $i$ leaves one subtree per edge, as removing a
site from the line left two half-lines, and the recursion of
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:lineschur} and~\eqref{eq:linecavity} becomes the two steps
of Sec.~\ref{sec:twosteps} with an edge for a complex,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{G}_{ii}^{-1}=\epsilon_{i}-z-\sum_{a\ni i}\Sigma_{a\to i},
  \qquad
  \Sigma_{a\to i}=t^{2}\,\mathcal{G}_{k\to i},
  \label{eq:treeG}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{G}_{k\to i}$ is the diagonal element at the other end of the
edge with $i$ removed, computed from $k$'s other edges by the same rule.
These are Eqs.~(3) and~(4) of \citet{tonetti2025}, and Eq.~(S11) of their
supplement. The down step is the interior of the complex --- a single
number, $t^{2}\mathcal{G}$ --- and the up step is a sum over complexes,
which is the linearity of the self-energy in the branches. The line is
the case $K=1$.

The name is the physics. Eq.~\eqref{eq:treeG} says that site $i$, as the
particle sees it, is a level at $\epsilon_{i}+\operatorname{Re}\Sigma_{i}$
with a width $\operatorname{Im}\Sigma_{i}$, where
$\Sigma_{i}=\sum_{a\ni i}\Sigma_{a\to i}$ is the self-energy: the branches
shift the level, by the particle's excursions into them and back, and
broaden it, by the rate at which a particle placed on $i$ leaks into them
and does not return. Everything that follows is about that width. A
finite one means the particle escapes to infinity and a vanishing one
means it does not, and since
$\operatorname{Im}\Sigma_{a\to i}=t^{2}\operatorname{Im}\mathcal{G}_{k\to i}$,
the width of a site is fed by the widths of its neighbours, which is what
makes it a message.

At $W=0$ the recursion closes on itself as it did on the line. Every site
energy is zero and every cavity element sees the same tree, so
$\mathcal{G}_{k\to i}$ is one number $\mathcal{G}$ fed by the $K$ edges
beyond $k$, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:treeG} with one edge removed reads
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{G}^{-1}=-z-Kt^{2}\mathcal{G},
  \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad
  \mathcal{G}(z)=\frac{-z+\sqrt{z^{2}-4Kt^{2}}}{2Kt^{2}},
  \label{eq:cleantree}
\end{equation}
the root with $\operatorname{Im}\mathcal{G}>0$ for $\operatorname{Im}z>0$.
At real $z=E$ the square root is imaginary exactly when $E^{2}<4Kt^{2}$,
so $\operatorname{Im}\mathcal{G}(E+\mathrm{i}0^{+})$ is nonzero on
$|E|\le2\sqrt{K}\,t$ and zero outside it. The diagonal element adds the
$(K+1)$th edge, $\mathcal{G}_{ii}^{-1}=-z-(K+1)t^{2}\mathcal{G}$, and its
imaginary part over $\pi$,
\begin{equation}
  \rho(E)=\frac{K+1}{2\pi}\,
  \frac{\sqrt{4Kt^{2}-E^{2}}}{(K+1)^{2}t^{2}-E^{2}},
  \qquad |E|\le2\sqrt{K}\,t,
  \label{eq:kestenmckay}
\end{equation}
is the density of states of the clean tree, and at $K=1$ it is
Eq.~\eqref{eq:linedos}. So the stationary states of
the tree of degree $K+1$ have energies in the band $|E|\le2\sqrt{K}\,t$
and nowhere else, and every one of them is extended: the fixed point with
a finite imaginary part is the only one inside the band, and there is
none outside it. The clean tree is the extended phase of the chapter's
own criterion, and the band is where the disorder of the next sections
will carve its mobility edge.

On a chygraph the up step is unchanged and the down step is a matrix
inverse. Let complex $a$ have members $i$ and $J=a\setminus i$, let $A_{J}$
be the adjacency of the clique among $J$, and let $\Sigma_{J\to a}$ be the
diagonal matrix of the members' cavity self-energies from their other
complexes. Then the self-energy the complex transmits to $i$ is a Schur
complement,
\begin{equation}
  \Sigma_{a\to i}(z)=t^{2}\,\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf T}
  \bigl[(\epsilon_{J}-z)-t A_{J}-\Sigma_{J\to a}\bigr]^{-1}\mathbf{1},
  \label{eq:schur}
\end{equation}
which is the interior of the complex summed exactly, in the only sense a
resolvent can be summed: every path from $i$ into the complex and back out
to $i$, through any of its members any number of times, with the members'
own branches folded into $\Sigma_{J\to a}$. At cardinality two $J=\{k\}$ and
Eq.~\eqref{eq:schur} is $t^{2}/(\epsilon_{k}-z-\Sigma_{k\to a})$, which is
Eq.~\eqref{eq:treeG}. For a triangle it is a $2\times2$ inverse in closed
form,
\begin{equation}
  \Sigma_{a\to i}=t^{2}\,\frac{m_{1}+m_{2}+2t}{m_{1}m_{2}-t^{2}},
  \qquad m_{j}=\epsilon_{j}-z-\Sigma_{j\to a},
  \label{eq:triangleG}
\end{equation}
with the $2t$ in the numerator the loop: the path from $i$ to $j_{1}$ to
$j_{2}$ and back, which is what the tree cannot have.

Two things follow. On any chygraph whose incidence structure is a tree
Eq.~\eqref{eq:schur} is exact, and Sec.~\ref{sec:loc-checks} confirms it to
$10^{-15}$ against direct inversion. And the cost is polynomial in the
cardinality --- a $(c-1)\times(c-1)$ inverse rather than a sum over $2^{c}$
states --- so the hard ceiling of Sec.~\ref{sec:notdone} does not apply to
this model: a complex of thirty members is a $29\times29$ matrix.

\paragraph{The ensembles.} The Bethe lattice is the regular chygraph with
every atom in three edges. Beside it this chapter puts the regular chygraphs
in which every atom belongs to $s$ single edges and $t$ triangles, written
$(s,t)$, so that $(3,0)$ is \citeauthor{tonetti2025}'s lattice and
$(1,1)$ has the same degree with one of the three neighbour pairs closed
into a loop; at degree four, $(4,0)$, $(2,1)$ and $(0,2)$. Holding the
degree fixed is Sec.~\ref{sec:threads}'s rule: the comparison is between
graphs with the same number of neighbours per site, differing in whether
those neighbours are neighbours of each other. The population dynamics
carries two populations of self-energies, one per cardinality, and an atom
left out of an edge draws $s-1$ edge messages and $t$ triangle messages,
left out of a triangle $s$ and $t-1$: the excess bracket of
Fig.~\ref{fig:themap}, once more.

The spectrum of the pure hopping problem on $(s,t)$ has a bottom $e_{\min}$
that the uniform fixed point of Eq.~\eqref{eq:schur} locates --- the largest
$E$ below which a real solution exists --- and an isolated eigenvalue
$-t(s+2t)$ carried by the uniform vector, as on any regular graph
\citep{biroli2010}: the state with the same amplitude on every site is an
eigenstate at $-t$ times the degree, one state in $N$, invisible in the
density of states but the lowest level there is. With disorder the bottom
of the spectrum is $E_{\min}(W)=e_{\min}-W/2$ once that lies below the
isolated eigenvalue, which for $(3,0)$ is $W>0.34$ and agrees with
\citeauthor{tonetti2025}'s $W_{\min}$; every disorder used here is above
it. That bottom is reached only on a region where every site energy is
near $-W/2$, which the uniform disorder makes rare. The states near
$E_{\min}$ live on such regions, are few, and are localised on them; this
is the tail of the spectrum the opening paragraph spoke of, and it is
what the disorder thickens into the localised skin of
Sec.~\ref{sec:loops}. Table~\ref{tab:andersonwc}
lists $e_{\min}$: $-2\sqrt2$ and $-2\sqrt3$ for the two trees, which is the
check, and lower for the triangle ensembles, since a triangle's lowest
eigenvalue is $-2t$ against an edge's $-t$.

\section{The landscape, by linearity}
\label{sec:landscape}
\index{localization landscape|textbf}

The Localization Landscape is $u=(\hat H-E_{\min})^{-1}\mathbf{1}$, the
solution of a linear equation with the spectrum shifted to make the
operator positive, and \citet{arnold2016} show that $1/u$ is an effective
confining potential: eigenstates of $\hat H-E_{\min}$ at energy $E'$ live
where $1/u\le E'$. The reason is a bound. Since
$\psi=(\hat H-E_{\min})^{-1}E'\psi$ and the resolvent at $E_{\min}$ has
no negative entries, $|\psi(i)|\le E'\,u_{i}\max_{j}|\psi(j)|$: where
$u$ is small no state of energy $E'$ can have much amplitude, so $1/u$
acts as a wall and $u$ is a map of the wells. Physically $u$ is the
response of the graph to a unit source on every site, a membrane under a
uniform load in \citeauthor{filoche2012}'s original picture, and it is
large where the particle can spread and small where the site energies
fence it in. Its $i$th component is a row sum of the resolvent at real
$z=E_{\min}$, where nothing needs regularising, and on the Bethe
lattice \citeauthor{tonetti2025} write it as $u_{i}=\mathcal{G}_{ii}\eta_{i}$
with $\eta_{i}=1+t\sum_{k}\mathcal{G}_{k\to i}\eta_{k\to i}$, their Eqs.~(5)
to~(7): the row sum of a branch is the row sum of its root times what the
root transmits.

The same linearity carries it to a complex. With $M_{a}$ the real matrix
inverted in Eq.~\eqref{eq:schur} at $z=E_{\min}$ and $\eta_{J\to a}$ the
vector of the members' cavity row sums,
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  \eta_{a\to i}&=t\,\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf T}M_{a}^{-1}\,\eta_{J\to a},\\
  \eta_{i}&=1+\sum_{a\ni i}\eta_{a\to i},
  \qquad
  u_{i}=\mathcal{G}_{ii}(E_{\min})\,\eta_{i},
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:landscape}
\end{equation}
where $\eta_{j\to a}=1+\sum_{b\ni j,\,b\ne a}\eta_{b\to j}$. On an incidence
tree this is exact, and on random instances of $3000$ atoms it reproduces
the direct solve to a relative error of $10^{-8}$ on the trees and
$3\times10^{-3}$ on the densest triangle ensemble, the residue being the
long loops of the instance. The landscape is smooth: $u$ at neighbouring
atoms is strongly correlated, because both are dominated by the same
branches, and Sec.~\ref{sec:lltperc} has to carry that correlation or it
gets the percolation threshold wrong.

\section{Landscape percolation as a dependent layer}
\label{sec:lltperc}
\index{dependent layer}

The LLT proposal is that a state at energy $E'=E-E_{\min}$ is extended iff
the set $\{i:u_{i}\ge1/E'\}$ percolates. The set is the region the state
is allowed into, the sites where the wall $1/u$ is below the energy, that
is, the wells of the landscape at that depth. A state confined to a
finite well is localised, and the proposal is that a state is extended
only if the wells at its energy connect across the graph, which is a
percolation question. That is site percolation with an
occupation that is not independent from site to site but a function of a
cavity field, in the spirit of the dependent layers of
Sec.~\ref{sec:joint}, with a threshold rule of the kind Sec.~\ref{sec:groupcontagion}
met: here the field is continuous, so the transition is continuous, and
\citeauthor{tonetti2025} find it mean-field, $\gamma=1$, on the tree. On the
chygraph the map is Part~II's,
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  p_{j\to a}&=\theta\bigl(u_{j}-1/E'\bigr)
  \Bigl[1-\prod_{b\ni j,\,b\ne a}(1-p_{b\to j})\Bigr],\\
  p_{a\to i}&=1-\prod_{j\in J}(1-p_{j\to a}),
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:lltmap}
\end{equation}
the second line because a clique's members are all adjacent to $i$, so $i$
reaches the infinite cluster through $a$ if any other member does. The
landscape $u_{j}$ in the first line is the full one --- it includes the
complex $a$ through which $j$ is reached, since $u$ is a property of the
site --- and the message $\Sigma_{a\to j}$, $\eta_{a\to j}$ that completes
it is computed from the other members' cavity data in the same draw.
Every population element is therefore a triple $(\Sigma,\eta,p)$ generated
from one cavity draw. Generating $p$ separately, with the same marginals,
moves the threshold by a tenth (Fig.~\ref{fig:andersonmech}), which is the
smoothness of $u$ at work: open sites are found next to open sites.

The threshold is located by the growth rate of a small $p$ under
Eq.~\eqref{eq:lltmap}, renormalised each generation, which is the
linearisation of Sec.~\ref{sec:threshold} done numerically because the
$\theta$ makes the joint law awkward to write. On $(3,0)$ at $W=1.5$ the
threshold is $E'_{c}=0.473$, that is $E_{c}^{\mathrm{perc}}=E_{\min}+E'_{c}=-3.106$
at the bottom of the band, which by the symmetry of the tree's spectrum is
$3.106$ at the top against \citeauthor{tonetti2025}'s $3.165$, two per cent
apart. The triangle ensembles are not bipartite and their spectra are not
symmetric, so everything here is done at the bottom of the band and
compared there.

\section{The mobility edge as a stability line}
\label{sec:mobility}
\index{mobility edge|textbf}
\index{de Almeida--Thouless line}

The localised phase is the fixed point of Eq.~\eqref{eq:schur} at real
$z=E$: every message real, the imaginary parts zero, no site with a width,
a particle placed anywhere with no rate of escape. The extended phase is
where that fixed point is unstable to an infinitesimal imaginary part.
The perturbation has a physical form. Give $z$ a small $\eta$, an
absorber at infinity that removes the particle at a vanishing rate, and
ask whether the widths it induces are amplified or damped as they
propagate inwards through Eq.~\eqref{eq:treeG}: amplified, and the
particle reaches infinity and the state is extended; damped, and the
widths vanish with $\eta$ and the particle stays. The criterion is the
one this book has used for every transition, the linearised map at the
trivial fixed point, applied to the imaginary parts with the real parts
frozen at their distribution. Writing $M_{a}$ for the
real matrix of Eq.~\eqref{eq:schur} and $v=M_{a}^{-1}\mathbf{1}$,
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{Im}\Sigma_{a\to i}=t^{2}\sum_{j\in J}v_{j}^{2}
  \sum_{b\ni j,\,b\ne a}\operatorname{Im}\Sigma_{b\to j},
  \label{eq:imlinear}
\end{equation}
a linear map with positive random weights: $t^{2}\mathcal{G}_{k\to i}^{2}$ on
an edge, which is \citeauthor{tonetti2025}'s Eq.~(S14), and
$t^{2}[(m_{2}+t)/(m_{1}m_{2}-t^{2})]^{2}$ and its partner on a triangle.
A weight is large where the neighbour is resonant, its level
$\epsilon_{k}+\operatorname{Re}\Sigma_{k}$ within about $t$ of $E$, and
small otherwise, so a width propagates along chains of resonant sites.
Because the weights have heavy tails the right growth rate is not that of
the mean, which a rare chain of exact resonances dominates, but of a
fractional moment, which weighs the rare chain against the typical one as
the transition does: the localised phase is stable iff
$\lambda(\beta)<1$ for some $\beta\in(0,1)$, where $\lambda(\beta)$ is the
growth factor per generation of $\langle(\operatorname{Im}\Sigma)^{\beta}\rangle$
under Eq.~\eqref{eq:imlinear}, and the transition is where
$\min_{\beta}\lambda(\beta)=1$ \citep{abouchacra1973}. That minimum sits at
$\beta=1/2$ on the tree by a symmetry of the kernel, and the population
estimate here scans $\beta$ over $0.4$ to $0.6$. This is the de
Almeida--Thouless argument of Sec.~\ref{sec:atline} with a fractional
power in place of the square: a perturbation of random size, transmitted
with a random factor, whose typical growth decides.

The estimate is biased by the population. At the band centre of $(3,0)$,
where the transition is at $W_{c}=18.17$ \citep{tikhonov2019,parisi2019},
$\lambda(1/2)$ at $W=18$ is $0.967$, $0.977$ and $0.986$ with $2\times10^{5}$,
$10^{6}$ and $3\times10^{6}$ elements, rising towards one as the heavy tail
of the moment is sampled better, and the threshold read from
$10^{6}$ elements is a few per cent low. At the bottom of the band the
bias is smaller: $(3,0)$ at $W=1.5$ gives $E_{c}^{\mathrm{loc}}=-3.106$
against \citeauthor{tonetti2025}'s $-3.152$. The comparisons that matter
below are between ensembles at the same population, where the bias is
common.

\section{What loops do}
\label{sec:loops}

Three figures carry the result; the numbers behind them are cached with
the probe's results and \code{figs/\allowbreak localization.\allowbreak py}
prints them. Both lines are energies at the
bottom of the band, a state being extended for $E>E_{c}^{\mathrm{loc}}$ and
the landscape percolating for $E>E_{c}^{\mathrm{perc}}$; the gap
$E_{c}^{\mathrm{perc}}-E_{c}^{\mathrm{loc}}$ is positive where the LLT misses
extended states and negative where it invents them. Seven of the thirty
points were computed twice from independent populations: the percolation
threshold repeats to $0.002$ and the mobility edge to $0.003$ at $W=1.5$
and $0.03$ at $W=6$, which are the error bars.

\paragraph{The lines, measured from the band edge.}
Figure~\ref{fig:anderson} plots both lines as their distance above
$E_{\min}$, which removes the $-W/2$ that every line shares and leaves what
the disorder does. Three things are visible at once. The mobility edge
climbs away from the band edge roughly linearly in $W$ on every ensemble:
the localised skin at the bottom of the band thickens with disorder. The
percolation line climbs faster on every ensemble, so the two cross where
they are going to cross and separate thereafter. On the degree-three tree
they start together, a gap of $0.006$ at $W=1.5$ against
\citeauthor{tonetti2025}'s $0.013$, which is their small-disorder
agreement; the double crossing they find there below $W\approx2.5$, at
the level of a hundredth, is below the resolution of this calculation. On
the degree-four tree, which they do not treat, the percolation line
starts below the mobility edge and crosses it once, near $W=4.3$. And the
triangle ensembles lie above the trees on both lines, further above on
the percolation line: at degree three the two mobility edges are within
$0.1$ of each other up to $W=3$ while the percolation lines are $0.1$ to
$0.5$ apart, and at degree four, at $W=12$, the three mobility edges
spread over $0.9$ and the three percolation lines over $1.5$. Both lines
move with the loops; the landscape's moves about twice as far.

\paragraph{The gap.} Figure~\ref{fig:andersongap} is the difference, and
it is the chapter's result in one panel. At every disorder but one --- the two degree-three ensembles tie at
$W=6$ --- the gap increases with the number of triangles at fixed degree,
and it does so by moving the crossing to weaker disorder: $W\approx4.3$ on the degree-four
tree, $3.0$ with one triangle per site, $1.7$ with two. Above the crossing
the ordering never changes --- $0.27$, $0.49$, $0.75$ at $W=6$ and $1.3$,
$1.5$, $1.9$ at $W=12$ --- and at degree three the cactus opens a gap of
$0.20$ where the tree has none. Then the cactus does something the tree
does not. At $W=9$ its curve stops: the imaginary parts decay at every
energy, the whole band is localised, and the landscape still percolates,
at $E_{c}^{\mathrm{perc}}=-2.58$ and, at $W=12$, $-2.29$. That is not a shift
of a line but a line drawn through a band in which nothing is extended,
and the LLT has no way of knowing it.

\paragraph{Why the cactus localises first.} Figure~\ref{fig:andersoncentre}
gives the reason, at the band centre where it is cleanest: the growth rate
$\lambda(1/2)$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:imlinear} against disorder for the five
ensembles, the transition being where it crosses one.
Table~\ref{tab:andersonwc} lists the crossings: $17.4$ on the degree-three
tree, $8.6$ on the cactus at the same degree; $32.3$, $26.2$ and $18.1$ at
degree four as edges become triangles. The last number is within a unit of
the degree-three tree's, and that is the mechanism in one line. It is not
a matter of walks on the graph --- walks that never turn back grow as
$2$ per step on any graph of degree three, triangles or not --- but of
the tree the recursion runs on. A site reached through an edge passes the
imaginary part on through $s-1$ edges and $t$ triangles; a triangle
reaches two sites, each of which passes it on through $s$ edges and $t-1$
triangles. So the numbers of edge and triangle messages grow by the matrix
\begin{equation}
  \begin{pmatrix} s-1 & 2s\\ t & 2(t-1)\end{pmatrix},
  \label{eq:branchingloc}
\end{equation}
whose leading eigenvalue is the branching of the incidence tree:
$2$ for $(3,0)$, $\sqrt2$ for $(1,1)$, $3$, $2.56$ and $2$ at degree four,
the column beside $W_{c}$ in Table~\ref{tab:andersonwc}, which it orders.
A triangle spends two of a site's neighbours on each other, and a message
that leaves the cactus's site through its edge and enters a triangle is
passed on to two sites that each have one edge to pass it on: $2$ per two
steps, $\sqrt2$ per step, and two triangles per site give
$\sqrt2\cdot\sqrt2=2$, the degree-three tree's growth. Fewer branches,
fewer independent routes for a width to escape along, and the graph
localises at a disorder the tree shrugs off. The landscape does not know this. It is
a static, positive solution of a shifted linear problem, for which the
loop is the $2t$ in the numerator of Eq.~\eqref{eq:triangleG}, one more
term in a self-energy, and nothing about branching.

\paragraph{The two mechanisms, checked.} Figure~\ref{fig:andersonmech}
shows the two criteria at work. On the left, $\lambda(1/2)$ against energy
at $W=3$ for the tree and the cactus: a smooth crossing of one, at
$0.78$ above the band edge on the tree and $1.02$ on the cactus, which is
the mobility edge as a stability line, with the cactus's localised skin
thicker by a quarter of a unit. On the right, the percolation growth
factor against $E'$ on the tree at $W=1.5$, with the $(\Sigma,\eta,p)$
triple drawn jointly and with $p$ drawn from separate members of the
population: the threshold moves from $E'=0.47$ to $0.52$, a tenth, which
is what the smoothness of $u$ is worth, and a calculation that treats the
landscape as independent site by site gets the LLT's own prediction wrong.

\paragraph{What this says about the proposal.} \citeauthor{tonetti2025}
found the two transitions apart on the tree and read the tree as the worst
case, the agreement being good in three dimensions and worse the fewer
the loops. On these graphs the shortest loops make it worse still, in one
direction: every triangle moves both lines towards the band centre and
the percolation line about twice as far, so the landscape's picture of
allowed regions that connect at a threshold is a worse guide to where the
states are the more the graph is clustered, until on the degree-three
cactus it percolates through a band with no extended state in it. The
only regime where the lines agree is the crossing, which the triangles
move to weaker disorder, and it is narrow.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:loc-conclusions}

A Green's function is a message like any other in this book: the recursion
is the two steps, the interior of a complex is summed exactly, and what
changes is that the sum is a Schur complement, polynomial in the
cardinality where every other chapter paid $2^{c}$. On that footing the
two problems of \citet{tonetti2025} close on any chygraph of cliques, and
on regular graphs of edges and triangles they answer the question their
paper raised. Loops separate the landscape percolation line from the
mobility edge rather than joining them, against what the tree being the
worst case would suggest: both lines move towards the band centre with
every triangle, the percolation line about twice as far, the gap grows
with the triangle count at fixed degree and disorder, and on the
degree-three cactus the landscape percolates through a band in which
nothing is extended. The mechanism is that a triangle cuts the branching
of the tree the imaginary parts propagate on, from $2$ to $\sqrt2$ at
degree three, while adding one term to a static self-energy, and a static
object cannot tell the difference.

What is not done is the critical behaviour, which \citeauthor{tonetti2025}
find to differ between the two transitions on the tree; nothing here
computes an exponent, and the lines are located to a few hundredths, the
mobility edge less well than the threshold. The population bias on the mobility edge is a few
per cent and common to the comparisons made. And the ensembles are
regular; a chygraph of the maximal cliques of a real network, with the
degree and cardinality distributions of Chapter~\ref{ch:data}, is the same
calculation with populations of populations by layer and is not run here.

\section{Checks}
\label{sec:loc-checks}

\code{figs/\allowbreak localization.\allowbreak py} runs, before it draws
anything: the cavity of Eq.~\eqref{eq:schur} against direct inversion of
$\hat H-z$ on finite incidence trees of edges and triangles, to $10^{-15}$;
the pure-hopping band bottoms of $(3,0)$ and $(4,0)$ against $-2\sqrt2$ and
$-2\sqrt3$. \code{statmech/\allowbreak probe/\allowbreak anderson.\allowbreak
py check} adds the random instances: on $600$ atoms the error of the
cavity resolvent falls from $4\times10^{-2}$ to $6\times10^{-6}$ as the
imaginary part of $z$ goes from $0.2$ to $3$, which is what long loops do
and short ones do not, and the landscape agrees with the direct solve to
$10^{-8}$ on the trees. The percolation threshold is located by renormalised
iteration of Eq.~\eqref{eq:lltmap}, whose subcritical side shows as
extinction --- the support of a small $p$ shrinks geometrically in a
finite population --- so the growth curve of Fig.~\ref{fig:andersonmech}
is measured instead from a few generations started at a uniform $p$,
which cannot go extinct and gives the same crossing. The two benchmarks
against \citet{tonetti2025} and the band-centre $W_{c}$ are the tests from
outside the formalism, and the population bias is quoted rather than
hidden. Everything in this chapter is
at the level of the mobility edge and the threshold; nothing here computes
a critical exponent, and \citeauthor{tonetti2025}'s finding that the two
transitions differ in class is taken from them, not reproduced.
