\chapter{Satisfiability}
\label{ch:satisfiability}
\index{satisfiability|textbf}

Given a formula in Boolean logic, is there any assignment of true and false to
its variables that makes the whole thing true? Cook and Levin showed,
independently, that this question is hardest-in-class in a precise sense: every
problem in NP reduces to it. Unlike most problems that carry that distinction,
it is also one that people put to computers by the million. A modern solver will
dispose of a formula with a million variables and then sit forever on one with a
few hundred, and what separates the two is not size. In the random ensemble it
is a phase transition, close enough in kind to the ones in the preceding
chapters that the same machinery can be pointed at it.

This chapter points it, and reports where it reaches and where it does not.

Satisfiability completes Part~III's third family and is the last of its model
chapters. It is also the one where the book's machinery is furthest from
sufficient, and saying exactly how far is most of what this chapter does.

The mapping is the easiest in the book: variables are atoms, clauses are
complexes, and a clause forbids exactly one assignment of its members. That is
Chapter~\ref{ch:colouring}'s hypergraph rule with one forbidden configuration
instead of two. But one feature does not carry over, and it decides everything.
Colouring's constraint is symmetric under permuting the colours, so the uniform
message is a fixed point and the threshold has a closed form.
A clause is not symmetric: it forbids a \emph{particular} assignment, the one
its signs pick out. The uniform message is not a fixed point, the disorder has
to be carried, and no closed form survives.

What does survive is a hard-field treatment --- Chapter~\ref{ch:hittingset}'s
warning propagation, on this chygraph --- and it produces one exact result, one
structural statement, and one honest failure. The exact result is the $2$-SAT
threshold. The structural statement is that for $k\ge3$ the trivial fixed point
is stable at \emph{every} clause density, so there is no linear instability at
all. The failure is that the hard field then goes on reporting nothing wrong
well past the point where the formula has stopped being satisfiable.

Those three are about $k$-SAT as the literature has it: a flat object, variables
below and clauses above. Sections~\ref{sec:nested}
and~\ref{sec:flatteningcost} use the depth a chygraph allows.
A complex may contain other complexes, so a clause may have clauses among its
members --- and the resulting formulas are ones that conjunctive normal form,
CNF, cannot hold without
manufacturing exactly the overlap Chapter~\ref{ch:overlap} is about. That is
the part of this chapter with no counterpart in the satisfiability literature,
and it is where the chapter stops being an exercise.

\section{Clauses as complexes}
\label{sec:clauses}
\index{conjunctive normal form|textbf}
\index{CNF|see{conjunctive normal form}}

Satisfiability was introduced in Sec.~\ref{sec:sat-intro}; here it is in full.
A $k$-SAT formula over $N$ Boolean variables is a conjunction of $M$ clauses,
each a disjunction of $k$ literals --- a variable or its negation. A formula
written that way, as an \textsc{and} of \textsc{or}s, is in \emph{conjunctive
normal form}, CNF. A clause is
satisfied unless every one of its literals is false, so each clause forbids
exactly one of the $2^{k}$ assignments of its variables. The control parameter
is the clause density $\alpha=M/N$.

The chygraph is immediate. Layer $0$ is the variables, as atoms; layer $1$ is
the clauses, as complexes of cardinality $k$. A variable's chy-degree is the
number of clauses it appears in, so for a random formula it is Poisson with mean
$\ave{\kappa}=k\alpha$: each clause holds $k$ variable slots and there are
$\alpha N$ clauses.

\paragraph{The one thing that is new.} Every complex in this book so far has
carried an interaction that treats its members alike. A clause does not. It
carries $k$ \emph{signs}, and the assignment it forbids depends on them. Two
clauses on the same $k$ variables can forbid different assignments, and in a
random formula they do.

That is quenched disorder living \emph{inside} a complex, and it is the first
time the book has met any. Chapter~\ref{ch:statmech} allowed it without
comment --- Eq.~\eqref{eq:emitted} takes $-\beta H$ as an argument and does not
ask whether it is the same for every complex --- but the consequence is real,
and Sec.~\ref{sec:whatclausetransmits} is it.

\section{What a clause transmits}
\label{sec:whatclausetransmits}

Write $u_{j}$ for the value of variable $j$ that falsifies its literal in clause
$a$. Then the interior sum of Eq.~\eqref{eq:emitted} removes a single
configuration, and
\begin{equation}
  \bar{Z}_{a\to0}(\sigma_{0})
    =1-\mathbf{1}[\sigma_{0}=u_{0}]\prod_{j\in a\setminus0}\eta_{j}(u_{j}).
  \label{eq:satZ}
\end{equation}
Compare Eq.~\eqref{eq:tauhyp}'s parent, $\bar{Z}=1-\prod_{j}\eta_{j}(\sigma_{0})$: the
same shape, with the product taken at a fixed assignment rather than along the
diagonal. Satisfiability really is colouring at $q=2$ with the symmetry broken,
and Eq.~\eqref{eq:satZ} is where the breaking enters.

The consequence shows before any analysis. Put every incoming message
at $\eta=1/2$. Then Eq.~\eqref{eq:satZ} emits $1-2^{1-k}$ on the falsifying
value and $1$ on the other: at $k=3$, $3/4$ against $1$. The emitted message is
\emph{not} uniform, so the uniform message is not a fixed point.

That single fact separates this chapter from the last one. In
Chapter~\ref{ch:colouring} the colour-permutation symmetry gave a uniform fixed
point, Schur's lemma reduced the linearised map to one number, and
Eq.~\eqref{eq:taucol} followed in three lines. Here the symmetry is broken
clause by clause. It is restored only \emph{on average} over the signs, which
means the object that has a symmetric fixed point is the distribution of
messages and not a message. There is no scalar to linearise, and no closed form
of the kind Eq.~\eqref{eq:taucol} provides.

\section{Warning propagation}
\label{sec:sat-wp}
\index{warning propagation}

What is available is the hard-field limit, which is Chapter~\ref{ch:hittingset}
transplanted. Keep only whether a message is a \emph{warning}: clause $a$ warns
variable $i$ that $i$ must satisfy $a$, because every other member of $a$ has
already been forced to falsify it. Everything else a message might have carried
--- how strongly a variable leans, at what temperature, with what weight --- is
thrown away, and what survives the discarding is a rule short enough to state in
four lines.

\begin{model}{Warning propagation}
\begin{rules}
\rl{1}{Initialise} Every clause begins by sending no warning to any of its
members.
\rl{2}{Clause to variable} A clause sends a warning to a member when all of its
\emph{other} members have been forced to the values that falsify it. The clause
then has exactly one way left to be satisfied, and the warning demands it.
\rl{3}{Variable to clause} A variable is forced to a value when the warnings
reaching it from its \emph{other} clauses demand that value and none demands the
opposite. A variable warned both ways is a contradiction rather than a forcing,
and passes nothing on.
\rl{4}{Repeat} Apply rules~2 and~3 until no message changes.
\end{rules}
\end{model}

What the rules produce is a fixed point, and it is a fixed point of a very
coarse object: a message is one bit, so the state of the whole calculation is
one number per inclusion rather than one function. The rest of this section is
that fixed point averaged over the ensemble.

Let $\eta$ be the probability that a clause sends a warning along an inclusion,
and $\pi$ the probability that a variable reached along one is forced to a given
value.

\emph{Down.} A clause warns $i$ only when all $k-1$ other members are forced to
falsify it, so
\[
  \eta=\pi^{\,k-1}.
\]

\emph{Up.} A variable is forced by the warnings arriving from its \emph{other}
clauses. Here the signs enter, and they enter once: a warning arriving at $j$
points at one of the two values, and the chance that it is the value falsifying
the clause under consideration is $1/2$. So with $\bar\Phi$ the excess
chy-degree generating function, the mean number of warnings arriving in the
relevant direction is $\ave{\kbar}\eta/2$, and for Poisson chy-degree
\[
  \pi=\bigl(1-e^{-\ave{\kbar}\eta/2}\bigr)\,e^{-\ave{\kbar}\eta/2},
\]
the two factors being ``at least one warning that way'' and ``none the other
way''. A variable receiving warnings both ways is a contradiction, not a
forcing. Putting the two steps together with $\ave{\kbar}=k\alpha$,
\begin{equation}
  \eta=\Bigl[\bigl(1-e^{-k\alpha\eta/2}\bigr)e^{-k\alpha\eta/2}\Bigr]^{k-1}.
  \label{eq:wp}
\end{equation} \code{figs/\allowbreak satisfiability.\allowbreak py:wp\_\allowbreak map} is Eq.~\eqref{eq:wp}, iterated monotonically from zero.

Equation~\eqref{eq:wp} is Chapter~\ref{ch:hittingset}'s
Eq.~\eqref{eq:hs} with two changes.

The first is the factor $1/2$, and it is all that negatable variables buy. A hyperedge in Chapter~\ref{ch:hittingset} constrains its members in one
way only; a clause can constrain them either way, so half the warnings a
variable receives are irrelevant to any given clause.

The second is a sign. Chapter~\ref{ch:hittingset}'s map was
order-\emph{reversing}, which is why it needed the $F\circ F$ bracket of
Sec.~\ref{sec:hard} and why plain iteration oscillated. Equation~\eqref{eq:wp}
is order-\emph{preserving}: more warnings force more variables, which send more
warnings. Monotone iteration from zero suffices, exactly as for
Chapter~\ref{ch:cover}'s leaf removal. The two hard-field problems in this book
sit on opposite sides of the sign test that Sec.~\ref{sec:fixedpoint} said would
decide which solver applies, and here is the second of them.

\section{Two-SAT is exact; above it there is no threshold at all}
\label{sec:twosat}

Linearising Eq.~\eqref{eq:wp} at $\eta=0$, with $\pi\simeq k\alpha\eta/2$ for
small $\eta$, gives
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial\eta'}{\partial\eta}\bigg|_{0}
    =\lim_{\eta\to0}\Bigl(\frac{k\alpha\eta}{2}\Bigr)^{k-1}\Big/\eta
    =\begin{cases}
       \alpha, & k=2,\\[2pt]
       0, & k\ge3.
     \end{cases}
  \label{eq:satslope}
\end{equation}

Equation~\eqref{eq:satslope} is Sec.~\ref{sec:twoconstraints-col}'s
observation a fourth time, in a different quantity. There the degenerate case was a complex of two
members, which has no room for a partial violation; here it is a clause of two
literals, which has no room for a partial forcing. Different quantity, same
degeneracy, and the same consequence --- that a great deal of what reads as
general theory is an artefact of working at two.

\paragraph{$k=2$.} The trivial fixed point loses stability at
\begin{equation}
  \alpha=1,
\end{equation}
and the non-trivial branch grows continuously out of it --- $\eta^{*}=0$ at
$\alpha=0.99$ and $0.0066$ at $\alpha=1.01$. That is the exact $2$-SAT
threshold, which is one of the few thresholds in this subject known rigorously.
It comes out of the general branching condition with no adjustment, and it is
the chapter's benchmark.

\paragraph{$k\ge3$.} The derivative is not small; it is \emph{zero}, at every
clause density. A clause needs $k-1$ warnings at once before it will emit one,
so a single warning cannot start anything, and $\eta\equiv0$ is stable however
many clauses one adds.

This is Sec.~\ref{sec:groupcontagion}'s exclusion, in its purest form. There a
group needed $r\ge2$ infected members and a single seed could not invade; here a
clause needs $k-1$ forced variables and a single warning cannot propagate. The
book has now met the same structure five times --- interdependent networks,
threshold contagion, the Potts model above $q=2$, the unanimity interaction of
Sec.~\ref{sec:unanimity}, and satisfiability above $k=2$ --- and this is the
extreme case, because there is no linear instability anywhere on the axis to
mistake for a transition.

The non-trivial branch arrives instead by a fold (Fig.~\ref{fig:sat}). At $k=3$
the map first touches the diagonal at $\alpha=5.39$; below that the only
solution is $\eta=0$.

The published thresholds are from \citet{montanari2008}. Read the last
column: the warning map first notices that something is wrong at a density
$1.26$ times the true one at $k=3$, and nearly three times it at $k=5$. Below
its fold, warning propagation reports no warnings, no contradictions and no
difficulty --- for formulas that are, with probability approaching one,
unsatisfiable.

That is the same defect as Sec.~\ref{sec:hardfail}, one family later and one
degree worse. There, collapsing the field onto an integer discarded an $O(1)$
part and returned a cover of one half where the answer was one third. Here it
discards the same kind of information and the answer is not merely wrong but
uninformative over a wide range of densities.

\paragraph{How much of that number to believe.} Less than the table's digits
suggest. Which parts are solid:

The two results of Sec.~\ref{sec:twosat} are linearisations, so they do not
depend on how a variable receiving contradictory warnings is counted. The fold
does. Under the strict convention used above --- contradictory warnings are a
contradiction, not a forcing --- the $k=3$ fold is $5.386$; counting instead the
\emph{net} warning field, so that opposing warnings partly cancel, it is
$4.667$. The numbers move by fifteen per cent and more.

What does not move is the direction. Under either convention the fold lies above
$\alpha_{s}$ at every $k$ tried: $4.667$ against $4.267$ at $k=3$, $11.83$
against $9.93$ at $k=4$, $27.91$ against $21.12$ at $k=5$. So the qualitative
statement is robust and the number is a bookkeeping choice, and the table above
should be read as the first and not the second.

\section{Clauses of clauses}
\label{sec:nested}
\index{clauses of clauses|textbf}
\index{complex!containing complexes}

Everything so far has been a two-layer object: variables at the bottom,
clauses above them, and that is where the satisfiability literature stops.
It is also where this book would stop if a chygraph were only a hypergraph
with a better name. It is not. A complex may contain other complexes, to any
depth, and satisfiability is a good place to see what that buys, because the
answer turns out to be a class of formulas that flat CNF cannot hold without
damage.

\paragraph{Three levels.} Layer $0$ is the variables. Layer $1$ is clauses
$C_{a}$, each a disjunction of $k_{1}$ literals. Layer $2$ is clauses $D_{b}$
whose members are a \emph{mix}: $k_{2}$ variables and one layer-1 clause, the
latter appearing negated,
\begin{equation}
  D_{b}=\neg C_{a}\ \vee\ \ell_{1}\vee\cdots\vee\ell_{k_{2}},
  \label{eq:nested}
\end{equation}
and the formula is the conjunction of the $D_{b}$. The layer-1 clauses are
sub-expressions, not constraints in their own right.

Equation~\eqref{eq:nested} has a reading that needs no logic at all: it is an
\emph{implication}. ``If any of these $k_{1}$ things holds, then at least one of
these $k_{2}$ others must.'' Conditional requirements of exactly that shape are
what dependency, configuration and scheduling problems are made of, and they are
what a CNF encoding has to be talked into.

\paragraph{What the layers do.} The mechanism is the book's, in miniature. A
layer-1 complex takes the messages from its $k_{1}$ variables and emits one
number: the probability that it is false, $\prod_{i}\eta_{i\to a}(u_{i})$. Its
$2^{k_{1}}$ interior configurations are summed and never mentioned again. The
layer-2 complex then consumes that number \emph{exactly as it would a
variable} --- Eq.~\eqref{eq:satZ} unchanged, with one of its literals happening
to be a complex rather than an atom.

That is the whole content of ``a complex whose vertices are complexes'', and
little had to be added. Nothing in
Chapter~\ref{ch:statmech} distinguished a member that is an atom from a member
that is a complex; Chapter~\ref{ch:percolation}'s bracket already handled
arriving from below and from above. The formalism was built for this case.

\paragraph{Why this is not CNF.} Distribute Eq.~\eqref{eq:nested}. Since
$\neg C_{a}=\bigwedge_{i\in a}\neg\ell_{i}$,
\begin{equation}
  D_{b}\ \equiv\ \bigwedge_{i\in a}
    \bigl(\neg\ell_{i}\vee\ell_{1}\vee\cdots\vee\ell_{k_{2}}\bigr),
  \label{eq:flatten}
\end{equation}
one clause of length $k_{2}+1$ for each variable of the sub-clause. So the
formula \emph{is} expressible in CNF --- and the encoding does something the
book has a name for. Any two of those $k_{1}$ clauses share all $k_{2}$ plain
literals. For $k_{2}\ge2$ they share two or more variables, which is precisely
Sec.~\ref{sec:price}'s condition for a chygraph to stop being treelike
(Fig.~\ref{fig:nested}). Flattening a nested clause manufactures exactly the
overlap that Chapter~\ref{ch:overlap} is about.

The alternative encoding, a Tseitin auxiliary variable $y\leftrightarrow C_{a}$,
does no better: it needs $k_{1}+1$ clauses all containing $y$, and the resulting
factor graph has four-cycles through $y$. Either way the CNF form has short
loops that the nested form does not.

\section{What flattening costs}
\label{sec:flatteningcost}
\index{overlap!between complexes}
\index{conjunctive normal form}

That is an argument about structure. Here is the number.

Warning propagation on the three-level chygraph carries two kinds of warning
where the flat problem carries one. A layer-2 clause can force a plain variable
member, as before; or it can force its \emph{sub-clause} member to be false ---
and a sub-clause forced false forces all $k_{1}$ of its variables at once. That
second channel has no counterpart in a flat encoding, where the $k_{1}$
forcings arrive as $k_{1}$ separate and supposedly independent events.

Take each sub-clause to be the antecedent of exactly one layer-2 clause, so
$\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}=\alpha$ and a sub-clause is forced false exactly when its
single parent says so. Write $\pi$ for the probability that a variable on the
cavity branch is forced to a given value, as in Sec.~\ref{sec:sat-wp}. Four
quantities close the system:
\[
  \begin{aligned}
    \mu&=\pi^{k_{2}}, &\qquad \gamma&=1-(1-\pi)^{k_{1}},\\
    \delta&=\mu, &\qquad \eta&=\pi^{\,k_{2}-1}\gamma.
  \end{aligned}
\]
Reading them across: $D$ forces its sub-clause false, $\mu$, when all $k_{2}$ of
its plain variables are forced; the sub-clause \emph{is} false, $\delta$, when
its one parent says so; it is true, $\gamma$, when some member of it is forced
the other way; and $D$ forces a plain variable member, $\eta$, when the other
$k_{2}-1$ are forced and the antecedent holds.

Warnings then arrive at a variable from both levels --- from layer-2 clauses
directly, and from layer-1 clauses that have been forced false and are passing
the forcing down to all of their members:
\begin{equation}
  \lambda=k_{2}\alpha\,\eta+k_{1}\alpha\,\delta,
  \qquad
  \pi=\bigl(1-e^{-\lambda/2}\bigr)e^{-\lambda/2},
  \label{eq:nestedwp}
\end{equation}
which closes the system on $\pi$ alone.

\emph{The check that the bookkeeping is right} is $k_{1}=1$. A sub-clause with
one member is a relay: $\neg C$ is then a single literal and
Eq.~\eqref{eq:nested} is an ordinary $(k_{2}+1)$-clause. The three-level system
must therefore reduce to the two-level one at $k=k_{2}+1$, and it does ---
identically, to the last digit of a double, at $k_{2}=2,3,4$ and every density
tried. A middle layer that holds nothing costs nothing. \code{figs/\allowbreak satisfiability.\allowbreak py:nested\_\allowbreak G} closes the four quantities on $\pi$, and returns the two-level map at $k_{1}=1$ to the last digit of a double.

The flat treatment of the same formula reads
Eq.~\eqref{eq:flatten} as $k_{1}\alpha$ worth of independent
$(k_{2}+1)$-clauses. It should agree with the nested calculation where the
flattening is harmless and disagree where it is not, and
Sec.~\ref{sec:nested} says where the boundary is: at $k_{2}=1$ the distributed
clauses share one variable and the factor graph is still a tree; at $k_{2}\ge2$
they share two or more and it is not.

That is exactly what happens.

\emph{At $k_{2}=1$ the two agree, and agree as an identity.} Both are linear at
the origin, and both give the threshold $k_{1}\alpha=1$: the nested map because
$\lambda\simeq k_{1}\alpha(k_{2}+1)\pi^{k_{2}}$ reduces to $2k_{1}\alpha\pi$ at
$k_{2}=1$, the flat one because $k_{1}\alpha$ independent $2$-clauses reach the
$2$-SAT threshold of Sec.~\ref{sec:twosat} at $k_{1}\alpha=1$. No approximation
has been made on either side.

\emph{At $k_{2}\ge2$ they part company}, and by an amount that grows with the
size of the sub-clause (Table~\ref{tab:nestedfold}).

\paragraph{What this is an instance of.} The discrepancy is
Chapter~\ref{ch:overlap}'s, arriving from an unexpected direction. There, a clustered graph's maximal cliques overlapped and the
chygraph could not see the loops between them. Here the overlap is not a
property of the data at all --- it is \emph{manufactured by the encoding}. The
formula has a perfectly treelike three-level representation; writing it in CNF
throws that away and creates the loops, and a flat treatment then pays for them.

The point is Chapter~\ref{ch:giant}'s, sharpened: the tree-based calculation is
not wrong, it is being applied to the wrong object. What is unusual about
satisfiability is that here one gets to \emph{choose} the object, and the
conventional choice is the bad one.

\section{Replica symmetry breaking}
\label{sec:sat-rsb}
\index{replica symmetry breaking}

Random $k$-SAT is the problem for which one-step replica symmetry breaking was
invented, and the reason is visible in the gap just measured.

What is needed is Sec.~\ref{sec:onestep}: a field replaced by a distribution
over fields, which for a warning-valued message is a distribution over warnings
--- a survey --- and propagating it is survey propagation
\citep{mezard2002science,mezard2002pre}, in the form
\citet{braunstein2005} state as an algorithm.

That calculation returns both of Sec.~\ref{sec:onestep}'s transitions, where
this chapter has been able to see neither. The solutions shatter at
$\alpha_{d}=3.86$, and they run out at $\alpha_{s}=4.267$ for $k=3$; between the
two lies the r\'egime with exponentially many clusters, and for $k\ge4$ a
condensation transition inside it \citep{montanari2008}. Rigorous bounds on the same
quantities, by an interpolation that does not assume the cavity ansatz at all,
are given by \citet{franz2003}.

So the position here is the same as in Chapter~\ref{ch:colouring} but more
acute. There, the replica-symmetric calculation gave a definite closed form that
was a local stability threshold rather than a colourability one. Here even that
is unavailable, because there is no symmetric fixed point to linearise about,
and the hard-field substitute is blind over a range that grows with $k$.

\paragraph{What the chygraph adds.} It adds the structure:
Eq.~\eqref{eq:wp} is written for an arbitrary chy-degree distribution, mixed
clause lengths are one layer per length, and correlations between a variable's
participation in short and long clauses are Sec.~\ref{sec:joint}'s joint
$\Phi$ --- none of which a fixed-$k$, Poisson treatment reaches. What it does
not add is the hard field's blindness, which is the previous section, and
Sec.~\ref{sec:sat-sp} is what removes it.

\section{One-step replica symmetry breaking}
\label{sec:sat-sp}
\index{survey propagation}
\index{replica symmetry breaking!one-step, satisfiability}

The map of Sec.~\ref{sec:sat-wp} is one term away from the object that does
work, and the last three sections have been about the gap that term leaves.

A warning is a fact about one state. Section~\ref{sec:onestep}'s survey is a
distribution over them, taken over the clusters, and here it is cheap for the
reason given there: the alphabet is finite, so the whole distribution is a
handful of numbers. The variable-to-clause object it needs has three components
rather than two --- forced to violate the clause, forced to satisfy it, and
neither. Equation~\eqref{eq:wp} already computes the first,
$\Pi^{u}=\pi$. Survey propagation divides it by the weight of all three,
\begin{equation}
  \eta=\Bigl[\frac{\Pi^{u}}{\Pi^{u}+\Pi^{s}+\Pi^{0}}\Bigr]^{k-1},
  \qquad
  \Pi^{0}=e^{-\ave{\kbar}\eta/2}\cdot e^{-\ave{\kbar}\eta/2},
  \label{eq:sp}
\end{equation}
the normalisation discarding the contradictory state and $\Pi^{0}$ being the one
the warning map has no room for --- a variable no cluster constrains.

\paragraph{What does not survive the step.} The scalar closure does not. Sections
\ref{sec:sat-wp} to~\ref{sec:satblind} could write one number for $\eta$ and
average over the ensemble, because a warning is present or absent and its mean
is all of it. A survey is not: $\eta$ becomes a continuous variable on
$(0,1)$ and its \emph{spread} enters the next update. Solving
Eq.~\eqref{eq:sp} as a scalar gives $\eta^{*}=0.1316$ at $k=3$,
$\alpha=4.267$, against $0.1828$ for the distribution --- $28$ per cent low, and
$8$ per cent low at $k=4$. So the step from Sec.~\ref{sec:sat-wp} to here is not
one of algebra but of what a message is, and it is why what follows is a
population and not a fixed point.

\paragraph{Counting the clusters.} The threshold is $\alpha_{s}$ and not
$\alpha_{d}$ --- the second of Sec.~\ref{sec:onestep}'s two transitions, not the
first --- so what locates it is the complexity and not the appearance of the
branch. Equation~\eqref{eq:sigmagen} at $m=0$ reads, on one layer of cardinality
$k$,
\begin{equation}
  \Sigma=\bigl\langle\ln Z_{i}\bigr\rangle
        +\alpha\bigl\langle\ln Z_{a}\bigr\rangle
        -k\alpha\bigl\langle\ln Z_{ia}\bigr\rangle,
  \label{eq:sigma}
\end{equation}
the three terms being a variable, a complex and an inclusion, evaluated on
surveys, with $\ave{k_{l}}/c_{l}=\alpha$ clauses and $\ave{k_{l}}=k\alpha$
inclusions per variable. Every one of them vanishes
identically at $\eta=0$, so $\Sigma$ says nothing about the trivial branch; it
counts the clusters of the other one, and $\Sigma=0$ is where there are none
left.

Table~\ref{tab:satsp} is the result: the one number in this chapter that comes
from outside the book and is reproduced. Sections~\ref{sec:twosat}
and~\ref{sec:satblind} bracket $\alpha_{s}$ --- from below by an exact
linearisation that stops at $k=2$, from above by a fold that overshoots by a
factor approaching three. Equation~\eqref{eq:sigma} reaches it.

\paragraph{What this does not settle.} Three things, and the chapter would be
overstating itself without them.

\emph{It is $m=0$.} Section~\ref{sec:onestep} says what that value of the Parisi
parameter is right for --- counting clusters of zero-energy solutions --- and
here is the thing it is wrong for: the condensation transition that sits between
$\alpha_{d}$ and $\alpha_{s}$ for $k\ge4$. That needs $m\ne0$, and this chapter
does not do it.

\emph{It is still treelike.} Equation~\eqref{eq:sp} inherits
Eq.~\eqref{eq:up}'s assumption unchanged: surveys from a variable's clauses are
combined as though those clauses met only at that variable.
Chapter~\ref{ch:overlap}'s obstruction is untouched by anything here. The
direction is favourable rather than otherwise --- Sec.~\ref{sec:flatteningcost}
showed that the flattening \emph{creates} the overlap, so the nested formulation
is the better founded of the two --- but a survey is no more exact on
overlapping complexes than a field is.

\emph{The structure is not what makes it work.} What
Eq.~\eqref{eq:sigma} adds over the published calculation is that it is written
for an arbitrary chy-degree distribution and an arbitrary interior, so the
nested clauses of Sec.~\ref{sec:nested} are a substitution rather than a
derivation. That the numbers come out right for flat $k$-SAT is a check on the
implementation, not a result.

\section{Checks}
\label{sec:sat-checks}

\checkhead{Known results}
Equation~\eqref{eq:satslope} at $k=2$ gives $\alpha=1$, the exact $2$-SAT
threshold, and the non-trivial branch is confirmed to be absent at
$\alpha=0.99$ and present at $\alpha=1.01$. Section~\ref{sec:sat-sp} returns
$\alpha_{s}$ at $k=3$ and $k=4$ to three tenths of a per cent, against
\citet{montanari2008}, with neither published value entering the calculation.
Every number in that section was already known, and reproducing it checks the
implementation rather than establishing anything.

\checkhead{New results}
The nested clauses of Secs.~\ref{sec:nested} and~\ref{sec:flatteningcost} are
the part of the chapter with no counterpart in the satisfiability literature,
and Table~\ref{tab:nestedfold} is the result. It is checked at the two places
where the answer is known independently. At $k_{1}=1$ a sub-clause with one
member is a relay, so the three-level system must reduce to the two-level one at
$k=k_{2}+1$, and it does --- identically, to the last digit of a double, at
$k_{2}=2,3,4$ and every density tried. At $k_{2}=1$ the distributed clauses
share one variable and the flattening is harmless, so the nested and flat
treatments must agree, and both give $k_{1}\alpha=1$ with no approximation on
either side. Underneath, Eq.~\eqref{eq:satZ} is checked against a direct
enumeration of a clause's $2^{k}$ assignments for $k=2,3,4$ in rational
arithmetic; the claim that the derivative at the trivial fixed point vanishes
for $k\ge3$ is checked as a \emph{rate}, the finite difference at $\varepsilon$
falling like $\varepsilon^{k-2}$ at $k=3,\dots,6$; and Eq.~\eqref{eq:sp} solved
as a scalar comes out low against the population by $28$ per cent at $k=3$ and
$8$ at $k=4$, which measures Sec.~\ref{sec:onestep}'s first cost rather than
asserting it.

\checkhead{Partial results}
The fold of Table~\ref{tab:satfold} is convention-dependent. Both conventions
for a contradicted variable are run; they agree at $k=2$, where the answer is a
linearisation, and disagree above it by fifteen per cent and more, though both
put the fold above $\alpha_{s}$. The chapter quotes the disagreement rather than
one convention's numbers alone, and the hard-field results of
Secs.~\ref{sec:sat-wp} to~\ref{sec:flatteningcost} are not satisfiability
thresholds except at $k=2$. The one-step calculation is at $m=0$, so the
condensation transition that sits between $\alpha_{d}$ and $\alpha_{s}$ for
$k\ge4$ is outside it, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:sp} inherits Eq.~\eqref{eq:up}'s
treelike assumption unchanged.
