\chapter{Chygraphs with meta-complexes}
\label{ch:metacomplex}

Chapter~\ref{ch:gbp}'s repair works by leaving the chygraph: it builds a region
graph beside the network and passes messages on that. There is a second repair
that does not leave it, and it uses a freedom the construction already has ---
a complex may contain complexes. Merge two complexes that share two or more
atoms into one, and the pairwise overlap is gone. Whether that is enough to
make the answer exact is measured in Sec.~\ref{sec:mergeerror}, and it is not
always.

\section{Merging complexes}
\label{sec:merging}
\index{merging complexes|textbf}

A more direct repair than a region graph: if two complexes share more than one
atom, stop treating them as two. Merge them into a single \emph{meta-complex} holding the
union of their atoms, and sum its interior exactly.

Nothing is added to the formalism to do this. A complex whose vertices are
complexes is what a chygraph is --- the freedom Chapter~\ref{ch:satisfiability}
used to nest clauses --- and Eq.~\eqref{eq:emitted} constrains only that the
interior can be enumerated, not its size or internal structure. On
Fig.~\ref{fig:sharededge}'s example the merge is immediate: the two triangles
become one complex of four atoms, its $2^{4}=16$ interior states are summed
exactly, and no overlap remains. Section~\ref{sec:twotriangles}'s discrepancy
disappears, by a route that needs no region graph and no parent-to-child
update.

So the repair removes the obstruction the part is named for. Whether that makes
it \emph{exact} is a second question, and the answer is no in general
(Sec.~\ref{sec:mergeerror}). What it then costs is a third
(Sec.~\ref{sec:mergecost}).

\paragraph{Merging is a percolation problem.} The cost is $2^{c}$ on the merged
complex, so the question is how large the merging grows.

Put a node on every complex, and a link between two complexes that share two or
more atoms. The meta-complexes are the connected components of that graph. The
repair is affordable exactly while that graph has \emph{no giant component} ---
and the moment it has one, the merging swallows a finite fraction of the network
into a single complex whose interior cannot be enumerated at any price.

Two details make the closure slightly more than one pass of connected
components. Merging is iterated: two meta-complexes assembled from disjoint
components can still share two atoms between their \emph{unions}, so the
procedure must be run to a fixed point. And the merged complex inherits every
inclusion of its parts, so its chy-degree is the sum --- which is what makes the
component grow.

\section{What the merged chygraph gets wrong}
\label{sec:mergeerror}

Merging removes every \emph{pairwise} violation by construction: run to a fixed
point, no two surviving complexes share more than one atom. That is the
condition Sec.~\ref{sec:treelike} states, and it is tempting to stop there and
call the result exact. It is not sufficient, and the reason is the one this part
opened with, one level higher again.

Pairwise intersections of at most one node do not make the incidence structure a
tree. Three or more complexes can meet in as many different single nodes and
close a cycle complex--node--complex--node--complex--node, and the chygraph
recursion is a Bethe calculation on that structure. Where such a cycle survives
the merge, so does an error.

The smallest example needs no merging at all, and is worth seeing before the
measurement (Fig.~\ref{fig:ring}). Glue $k$ triangles in a ring, each meeting
the next at one vertex. Every pair of complexes shares exactly one node, so the
condition of Sec.~\ref{sec:treelike} is satisfied --- and the incidence
structure is a ring of $2k$ steps, not a forest.

Figure~\ref{fig:coremeta} says the same thing as a quantity rather than a test.
Before merging, not one of the $120$ instances is core-free: propagation on the
clique chygraph is left with between a half and three quarters of the atoms it
cannot resolve (Fig.~\ref{fig:corebonds}). After merging the core is gone on
$100$ of them --- every hyperbolic instance, every real one, a third of the
placed ones --- and those hundred are precisely the hundred the recursion solves
exactly. A core of zero and an acyclic incidence structure are the same
statement, one counted and one tested, and either can be read off before the
messages are run.

What survives on the twenty placed instances that keep a core is small, and it
is one thing rather than many (Fig.~\ref{fig:coreexample}). Every one of the
twenty is left with a \emph{single} connected cluster --- median four atoms
across four complexes, never more than ten across nine --- and none is left with
two. That is structural rather than lucky: the closure has already absorbed
every pair of complexes sharing two atoms, so whatever survives is joined at
single atoms, and a single atom joining two otherwise separate pieces is a cut
vertex, which leaf removal strips. Anything that would have separated the
residue has been removed by the time the residue exists.

Half of the twenty carry one independent cycle and are the minimal ring of
Fig.~\ref{fig:ring} with $k=3$: three complexes, three atoms, six steps. The
other half carry two to five interlocking cycles in one tangle. So the
obstruction is not diffuse damage spread over the instance. It is one small
knot, and the whole of the error in $\ln Z$ comes from it.

The correspondence is exact and it goes both ways. All $200$ runs whose merged
incidence structure is acyclic come out exact, to $5\times10^{-13}$ at worst.
All $40$ that retain a cycle come out wrong, by $5\times10^{-3}$ to $2.25$ in
$\ln Z$ with a median of $0.18$. Not one instance falls on the wrong side. The
condition for the meta-complex chygraph to be exact is that the merged incidence
structure is a forest, and merging to a treelike family does not deliver it.

The classes divide sharply. On hyperbolic random graphs and on real
neighbourhoods the merge closure is exact everywhere --- largely because it has
swallowed the instance whole (Table~\ref{tab:vs}), and a single complex has no
incidence structure left to close a cycle in. On the Karrer--Newman ensemble
only a third of the merged families are acyclic, and the other two thirds keep
an error.

That is not merging failing where the plain calculation would have done better.
Merging is a generalisation of it --- with nothing to merge the two coincide ---
and on these instances it takes the median error from $0.289$ to $0.033$. It
improves the answer by a factor of ten and stops short of exactness, because the
loops it leaves are the ones of Fig.~\ref{fig:ring}: running between complexes
rather than inside a shared pair of atoms, where merging has no purchase. The
ensemble on which the closure is cheap is the ensemble on which it leaves those
loops, and for one reason --- it leaves many small meta-complexes rather than
one large one, and many small complexes are what closes a cycle.

Figure~\ref{fig:mergeerror} makes the mechanism visible. A cycle needs at least
three complexes meeting in at least three distinct single nodes, so a closure
leaving three meta-complexes or fewer cannot have one --- and in the whole
sample, none does: every such family is exact at machine precision. Cycles begin
at four, and the error appears with them. What decides exactness is therefore
not how large the largest meta-complex is, which is what
Sec.~\ref{sec:mergecost} prices, but how \emph{many} the closure leaves. The two
run in opposite directions, and a closure cheap enough to enumerate is for that
reason the more likely to have left a cycle behind.

verdict above is
about hyperbolic random graphs, not about clustering. Section~\ref{sec:karrer}'s

the closure comes out the other way.

The count is flat in $n$ on the placed ensemble and grows with $n$ on the
geometric one, and the mechanism is a one-line estimate. Two motifs meeting at a
vertex share a \emph{second} vertex only if their remaining members happen to
coincide, which for a random matching has probability $O(1/n)$. There are $O(n)$
such meeting pairs. The product is $O(1)$: a placed ensemble contains a number
of edge-sharing motif pairs that does not grow with the network at all. Divided
by the $\Theta(n)$ intersecting pairs that is
$\mathrm{shared}_{2+}\sim1/n$ --- measured at $0.0070$, $0.0021$, $0.0013$ and
$0.0007$ over the first block of the table, a slope of $-0.99$ against $\log n$.
A hyperbolic graph has no such suppression, because its overlaps are not
coincidences: the geometry puts every clique in a neighbourhood of others.

\paragraph{An ensemble built out of overlaps.} \citeauthor{karrer2010} raise
the case themselves. Their edge-triangle model, they note, is unrealistic in the
way that matters here --- real triangles share edges and theirs do not --- and
their remedy is to promote the shared-edge pair to a subgraph type of its own: a
diamond on four vertices, placed like any other element. That is
Sec.~\ref{sec:merging}'s merge, performed when the ensemble is written down
instead of afterwards.

So run the closure on such a graph, blind, with no knowledge of what was placed.
Its maximal cliques are the two triangles of each diamond, which \emph{do} share
an edge, so here $\mathrm{shared}_{2+}$ does not vanish with $n$: it sits at
$0.06$, by design. The closure terminates anyway, at a largest meta-complex of
$21$ atoms at $n=1000$ falling to $10$ at $n=8000$ --- and what it returns are
the placed diamonds, $78.5$ per cent of them recovered exactly at $n=1000$ and
$89.0$, $93.8$ and $97.2$ per cent at $2000$, $4000$ and $8000$. The shortfall
is the same $O(1)$ residue, about a hundred elements at every size, a fifth of
them at the smallest and a thirtieth at the largest. On this ensemble the merge
recovers the modelling decision that produced the ensemble.

\paragraph{A finite-size caveat.} The $O(1)$ constant grows
with motif density, and while it is finite it need not be small. At
$\ave{k}\simeq17$ --- eight triangles per vertex --- the accidental overlaps are
numerous enough to percolate the overlap graph at small $n$: essentially the
whole network is one meta-complex at $n=1000$ and $2000$, a fifth of it at
$4000$, and then the closure collapses to $19$ atoms at $8000$ and $13$ at
$16\,000$. The asymptotics win, and they win abruptly, but the size at which
they take over grows with the density. A measurement at one $n$ does not decide
this; the slope against $n$ does (Fig.~\ref{fig:motifs}).

The messages are cheaper everywhere, by five to thirteen orders of magnitude in

The row that settles the question is the last of each block. On all eleven
hyperbolic runs where GBP reaches no sound fixed point, the closure swallows the
entire instance: $c=n$, and merging has degenerated to enumerating the graph. It
does so on $27$ of the $34$ real neighbourhoods and $7$ of the $15$ placed ones.
The two repairs are not complementary and merging is not a fallback for the
messages --- the same dense, tangled overlap defeats both, and it defeats
merging first. Where GBP has a sound fixed point the closure is already paying
$2^{13.6}$ against $2^{8.1}$ on the real neighbourhoods; where GBP has none, it
is paying $2^{n}$.

The placed ensemble is the exception that shows the rule. It is the one class
where the closure mostly does \emph{not} degenerate --- $2^{10.7}$ against GBP's
$2^{3.3}$, and only one of its $43$ sound runs swallows the instance --- and it
is also the class whose overlaps are coincidences of a random matching rather
than a feature of the structure. Merging survives exactly where there is little
to merge.

\paragraph{What merging has that the messages do not.} Two things, and neither
is cost.

The first is that its guarantee is unconditional. Where the closure terminates
at an enumerable size the answer is exact, and there is no iteration to
converge, no fixed point that can be inconsistent, and no counting scheme that
can come out worse than doing nothing --- the three ways
Secs.~\ref{sec:sixty} to~\ref{sec:realoverlap} watched GBP fail. The cost is
also known before any work is done: build the overlap graph, take components,
read off $c$.

The second is the one that matters for the rest of the book. A merged complex is
a complex. Nothing leaves the formalism, Eq.~\eqref{eq:emitted} applies to the
meta-complex exactly as it applied to its parts, and a chygraph whose complexes
have been merged is a chygraph, which is an object this book knows how to treat
as an ensemble. A region graph is not: its regions are an explicit list drawn
from one network and its messages are functions on them.
Section~\ref{sec:whatwouldclose} is the statement of what would have to be
supplied to lift them, and it is not supplied here. So the choice between the
two repairs is not settled by Table~\ref{tab:vs}: on one instance the messages
are cheaper and reach further, and merging is the one of the two that is still
a chygraph afterwards.

\paragraph{The condition.} Neither that clustering is harmless nor that the
merge always works: the condition is where the overlaps come from.

Where the dense elements are \emph{specified} --- a modeller choosing which
motifs go in, or data that records its groups directly, which is
Sec.~\ref{sec:layers-data}'s route (a) --- the ensemble is treelike above the
level of those elements by construction, the overlaps that remain are accidents
of finite size, and the closure terminates. Where they are \emph{emergent} --- a
geometry or a growth rule producing whatever motifs it produces --- the overlaps
arrive at every scale and their number is extensive. Clustering is compatible with either, which is why
Chapter~\ref{ch:data} measures $\mathrm{shared}_{2+}$ on real data rather than
inferring it from the clustering coefficient.

The test is one line. Measure the offending-pair count at two sizes: if it is flat, merge; if it grows like $n$, do not, and
Sec.~\ref{sec:whatwouldclose} is the route that remains.

\paragraph{Three consequences.} The repair is exact where it terminates: a
complex is allowed to contain complexes, so absorbing an overlap into a larger
interior is a legal move, and it gives the answer with no approximation and no
region graph to build.

The threshold that decides it is a percolation threshold on the overlap graph,
which is what Chapter~\ref{ch:percolation} computes. The feasibility of the
repair is therefore a Part~II calculation, answerable for a given ensemble
before the merge is attempted.

On hyperbolic random graphs it terminates only in the very sparse régime, on
the placed ensemble at any density measured, and on none of the six real
networks. Merging and
generalised belief propagation are the two ends of one dial --- merge
everything and the messages become trivial but the interior impossible; merge
nothing and the interior is easy but the messages approximate --- and where to
sit on it is an ensemble-by-ensemble question.
Section~\ref{sec:whatwouldclose}'s route is what remains where merging does not
terminate.

\paragraph{On a given instance.} Between those extremes merging can still work
on the instance at hand rather than on the ensemble. Which complexes to merge is
a mechanical, checkable operation: build the overlap graph, take components,
verify no two survivors share two atoms, verify each is small enough to
enumerate. A tool taking a network, proposing a merge closure and reporting the
largest interior it produces answers in one pass whether the exact route is
open, as a number.


\section{The hierarchy of region levels}
\label{sec:hierarchy}

A graph cannot see a triangle. Promote triangles to complexes and the chygraph
cannot see two triangles sharing an edge. Promote \emph{those} pairs to regions
and the region graph cannot see whatever loops the regions form among
themselves. Each level removes the obstruction below it and exposes the one
above. That is the cluster variation method, and the chygraph is one rung of it
made systematic and given an ensemble.

Whether the hierarchy is a solution or a regress turns on whether it
\emph{terminates} for a given ensemble --- whether, for maximal cliques of a
hyperbolic random graph, some finite level captures essentially all of the
correlation. Section~\ref{sec:sixty} constrains the answer: the instance
calculation is exact exactly when the clique structure is chordal, so the
question becomes the distribution of chordality-violating motifs in the
ensemble and how fast their contribution decays. That is not answered here.


\section{The ensemble-level problem}
\label{sec:whatwouldclose}

The missing step can be stated precisely.

Every other calculation in this book carries a message per chy-degree
\emph{class}: a distribution over the ensemble, not a function on one object.
Closing the overlap deficit means lifting the parent-to-child update to a
message indexed by region \emph{type}, over a region graph that is itself a
random object. Two things would have to be supplied. What is the distribution of
intersection patterns among the maximal cliques of the ensemble --- how often do
two cliques share an edge, three cliques a vertex, and so on? And what does the
parent-to-child update become when averaged over that distribution? The
message-passing treatments that keep short loops explicitly
\citep{cantwell2019} are the natural point of comparison.

The instance calculation also constrains what an ensemble treatment must
handle. Six of twenty non-chordal hyperbolic instances do not converge at
damping $0.999$, and on the real neighbourhoods of Sec.~\ref{sec:realoverlap}
$34$ of $64$ yield no usable fixed point. An ensemble treatment inherits that
instability --- averaging does not make a divergent iteration converge --- so
damping is not enough; it needs a schedule or a different parameterisation of
the message.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:part4-conclusions}

\paragraph{On real networks the repair works.} That is the result to take from
the part, and it is the case the book is about. Chapter~\ref{ch:overlap}
measured the cavity method erring by a median of $5.06$ in $\ln Z$ on the $120$
real neighbourhoods --- a factor of $150$ in $Z$ --- because their complexes
share more than one atom. Merging those complexes and solving the resulting
chygraph removes the error entirely: all $120$ come out exact, the worst
discrepancy being $3\times10^{-13}$, which is the arithmetic and not the
approximation. The same holds on all $60$ hyperbolic instances. Two of the three
classes are repaired completely by a construction the chygraph already
contained.

\paragraph{And it works for a reason that can be stated.} The chygraph recursion
is exact when the incidence structure of its complexes is a forest. Merging does
not guarantee that --- it removes pairwise overlap, and three complexes can
still close a cycle through three single nodes --- but on real neighbourhoods it
delivers it every time, because the closure there merges nearly everything into
one to three large complexes and a large complex absorbs the loops into an
interior that is then summed exactly. Over all $240$ runs the criterion is
exact in both directions: every one of the $200$ acyclic families is exact,
every one of the $40$ cyclic ones is not, by $5\times10^{-3}$ to $2.25$ in
$\ln Z$. Nothing falls on the wrong side.

\paragraph{On the third class it helps without finishing the job.} Merging is a
generalisation of the plain clique calculation, not an alternative to it: where
no two complexes share two atoms the closure does nothing and the two coincide.
So it cannot fail where the clique calculation succeeds, and on the
Karrer--Newman instances it does not fail --- it takes the median error from
$0.289$ to $0.033$, a factor of ten. What it does not do there is reach
exactness, and Fig.~\ref{fig:ring} is why.

That ensemble is built from two independent matchings: triangles, placed by
matching corners in triples, and single edges, placed by matching stubs in
pairs. Two placed triangles share an edge only when their remaining corners
coincide, probability $O(1/n)$, so there is very little pairwise overlap --- and
very little for the closure to remove. It returns seven meta-complexes where the
other two classes leave two or three, about four of them bare single edges; not
one bare edge in the sample comes from a placed triangle, whose three edges
always lie inside it. Those stubs form a sparse random graph carrying its own
loops, and the loops run \emph{between} complexes --- triangle, node, stub,
node, triangle --- exactly as in Fig.~\ref{fig:ring}. Merging removes a loop only
when it lies inside a shared pair of atoms, so it cannot reach these. The
construction that gives the ensemble its small pairwise overlap is the one that
leaves its loops where merging cannot go, and cycles appear as soon as the
closure leaves four meta-complexes.

One consequence should be recorded rather than smoothed over. Because the Bethe
free energy is not variationally bounded, a generalisation need not improve
every instance: over the $240$ runs merging improved $224$ and made $16$ worse,
all sixteen Karrer--Newman and all with a cyclic incidence structure, typically
by a factor between $1.2$ and $2.5$. Absorbing some of the loops into interiors
moves a loopy fixed point, and the direction is not guaranteed.

\paragraph{What it costs, and where that bites.} The repair is exact where the
incidence structure is a forest, but $2^{c}$ on the largest meta-complex. On
neighbourhoods of twenty vertices that is affordable by construction. On whole
real networks it is not: Table~\ref{tab:mergereal} finds a closure that
terminates on none of the six, the smallest meta-complex any of them admits
being $53$ atoms. So the repair is exact and available locally, and the question
it leaves is not accuracy but reach.

\paragraph{The calculation is one routine.} Both the failure of
Chapter~\ref{ch:overlap} and the repair here are computed by the same solver,
\code{ChygraphBP} in \code{probe/cavity\_clique.py}: belief propagation on the
atom--complex incidence structure, each complex summing its own interior over
the bonds that lie inside it, with $\ln Z$ read off the Bethe free energy in its
explicit form. Chapter~\ref{ch:overlap} runs it on the maximal cliques and
Sec.~\ref{sec:mergeerror}, through \code{probe/merge\_lnz.py}, runs it on the
merged family. Nothing else changes between the two numbers, which is what makes
them comparable. The solver is checked against exact enumeration on chygraphs
where the assumption holds --- chains, stars, trees of triangles --- and
reproduces them to $2\times10^{-15}$.

\paragraph{What remains open.} All of this is instance-level. It takes an
explicit list of complexes and an explicit interaction, where every other
calculation in the book carries a message per chy-degree class. The ensemble
version --- a message indexed by region type, over a region graph that is itself
a random object --- is not supplied here, and Sec.~\ref{sec:whatwouldclose}
states as precisely as this book can what it would take.


\section{Checks}
\label{sec:merge-checks}

\checkhead{Known results}
That a complex may contain complexes is the definition of
Chapter~\ref{ch:chygraphs}, not a new construction, and
Eq.~\eqref{eq:emitted} applies to a meta-complex unchanged. The
Karrer--Newman ensemble is \citeauthor{karrer2010}'s, and the implementation is
checked by recovering the elements it was told to place: run blind on a graph
built from diamonds, the closure returns $78.5$ per cent of the placed diamonds
at $n=1000$ and $97.2$ per cent at $n=8000$.

\checkhead{New results}
Where merging stops being affordable, on all three classes, is new, as is the
error it leaves and the head-to-head against the messages. The exactness test is
made against enumeration, and the acyclicity of the merged incidence structure
is tested directly rather than inferred from the pairwise condition; the two
disagree on $40$ of $240$ runs, which is the point of
Sec.~\ref{sec:mergeerror}. One trap is recorded because it was fallen into: a
maximal-clique family filtered to cliques of two or more atoms silently drops
isolated vertices, and the resulting chygraph is short by exactly $\ln 2$ per
dropped vertex --- an artefact indistinguishable, at a glance, from a real
error. The closure is iterated to a fixed point
rather than run once, because two meta-complexes assembled from disjoint
components can still share two atoms between their unions; the loop runs until a
pass changes nothing. The cost comparison of Table~\ref{tab:vs} is made on the
same instances the messages were run on, regenerated from the same seeds, so the
two columns describe one object rather than two samples.

\checkhead{Partial results}
Section~\ref{sec:placed}'s $O(1)$ overlap count is an estimate supported by a
measurement flat over an eightfold range in $n$, not a theorem: it is not proved
that the count is bounded for every motif set, and at high density the
finite-size crossover is late enough to invert the answer at $n=4000$. The two
ensembles compared there are matched on the clustering coefficient and on
nothing else, which is the comparison the section needs but leaves the degree
distributions unmatched. Table~\ref{tab:vs}'s verdict is drawn at $n\le20$,
where the whole instance is enumerable and the closure can therefore never cost
more than $2^{n}$; it establishes that merging degenerates where the messages
fail, not how the two scale. And the whole part is instance-level: the ensemble
version of Sec.~\ref{sec:whatwouldclose} does not exist, and
Chapter~\ref{ch:outlook} lists it with the other things this book does not do.
